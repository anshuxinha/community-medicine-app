OVERVIEW OF THE CHAPTER
Meaningful comparison examines the additional costs that one programme imposes over another, compared with the additional effects it delivers. That incremental view is the decision rule of cost-effectiveness and cost-utility analysis.

Compare two programmes by asking two questions together: how much extra money does the new one need, and how much extra health does it give? The incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER), the cost-effectiveness plane, and the threshold are built to answer those questions. The threshold is an estimate of opportunity cost, not a moral price of a life.

DEFINITIONS
INCREMENTAL COST-EFFECTIVENESS RATIO (ICER): The extra cost of one alternative over another, divided by the extra effect. ICER = (C1 − C0) / (E1 − E0), where 1 is the new (or more intensive) option and 0 is the comparator. Effects may be life-years, cases, or quality-adjusted life-years (QALYs). The same algebra with QALYs is the incremental cost-utility ratio; examinations still call it an ICER.

In words: what extra must be paid for each extra unit of health?

COST-EFFECTIVENESS THRESHOLD: The ICER at which the extra health from a programme is judged just worth the extra cost, because it equals the health that would be displaced if those resources were taken from existing care. It is an estimate of opportunity cost inside a constrained budget.

Think of the threshold as the extra health you would lose if you took money away from existing services to fund the new programme. If the new programme buys less health than that, it should not displace current care.

NET MONETARY BENEFIT (NMB): Effect × threshold minus cost. Positive NMB means the programme is cost-effective at that threshold.

NET HEALTH BENEFIT (NHB): Effect minus (cost / threshold). The same rule expressed in health units.

NMB converts health into money at the threshold and then subtracts cost. NHB converts cost into the health that money could have bought elsewhere, and then subtracts that from the health the programme actually gives. Both answers should point the same way.

[LAQ]Interpreting cost-effectiveness results for a health programme[/LAQ]

INCREMENTAL, NOT AVERAGE
The average ratio (cost of A / effect of A) compares A with doing nothing at zero cost and zero effect. That is the wrong comparison if current practice already costs money and already produces health. Always subtract the comparator.

If a district already runs a programme, the relevant question is not "what does the new programme cost per unit of health from scratch?" The relevant question is "compared with what we already do, how much extra money, and how much extra health?"

Example algebra (numbers are for method, not for citation as Indian policy). Comparator cost 14,200, effect 10.93 QALYs. New strategy cost 15,250, effect 12.66 QALYs. Extra cost 1,050. Extra QALYs 1.73. ICER ≈ 607 per QALY. A further, more expensive strategy is then compared with the new strategy, not with doing nothing.

COST-EFFECTIVENESS PLANE
Plot extra effect on the horizontal axis and extra cost on the vertical axis. The origin is the comparator.

| Quadrant | Extra effect | Extra cost | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|
| I (north-east) | Higher | Higher | Need a threshold: is extra effect worth extra cost? |
| II (south-east) | Higher | Lower | Dominant: accept (more health, less money) |
| III (south-west) | Lower | Lower | Need a threshold: is the saving worth the health given up? |
| IV (north-west) | Lower | Higher | Dominated: reject |

The origin is "what we do now." Moving right means more health. Moving up means more cost.

- North-east: better health, but more expensive. A threshold is needed before saying yes.
- South-east: better health and cheaper. Accept it. This is strong dominance in favour of the new option.
- South-west: cheaper, but worse health. A threshold is needed before saying that the saving is worth the health given up.
- North-west: worse health and more expensive. Reject it.

A dashed threshold line through the origin with slope equal to the threshold (cost per unit effect) splits quadrant I (and III). Points below the line in quadrant I are cost-effective at that threshold. Points above are not.

Draw a straight line from the origin whose slope is the maximum extra cost you are willing to pay for one extra unit of effect. In the north-east quadrant, a point below that line means the extra health is cheap enough to buy. A point above the line means the extra health costs more than the threshold, so the same money would buy more health if left in existing care.

DOMINANCE
Strong dominance: an option is both more costly and less effective than another. Drop it.
Extended (weak) dominance: an option is more costly per extra effect than a mixture of two others. In a league table of several alternatives, drop extendedly dominated options before computing ICERs along the efficient sequence.

Strong dominance is the north-west case: worse and more expensive. Extended dominance is less obvious. If option B sits between A and C, but costs more per extra unit of effect than moving from A to C, drop B. Mixing some of A with some of C would give more health for the same extra money than using B. After dropping dominated options, calculate ICERs only along the remaining efficient sequence, each time against the next cheaper undominated option, not against doing nothing.

Uncertainty means a result rarely sits in the exact centre of a quadrant. Confidence ellipses and cost-effectiveness acceptability curves (CEACs) describe the probability that an option is cost-effective as the threshold varies. Name them in a long answer; do not invent a curve in a short note.

Costs and effects are estimated with error, so the ICER is not a single certain number. A confidence ellipse is a cloud of likely extra-cost and extra-effect pairs around the point estimate. A cost-effectiveness acceptability curve plots, for each possible threshold, the probability that the new option is the cost-effective choice. In a long answer, name these tools. In a short note, stating that uncertainty should be reported is enough.

THRESHOLD AS OPPORTUNITY COST
A threshold is not "what society thinks a life is worth" in the abstract. Under a fixed health budget, spending more on programme A means spending less on programme B. The health lost in B is the opportunity cost. If the extra health from A is smaller than that loss, A should not displace B.

Indian context:
India does not publish a single statutory cost-per-QALY threshold of the kind used by the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) for all public purchasing. Health Technology Assessment in India (HTAIn) still compares extra cost with extra effect. District decisions use the same logic in natural units: extra rupees per extra child immunised, per extra sputum-positive patient treated, per extra controlled hypertensive, judged against other uses of National Health Mission (NHM) funds.

Do not quote a rupee-per-QALY cut-off as if it were an Indian law.

There is no official rupee figure that can be written as the Indian cost-per-QALY threshold. HTAIn still asks the incremental question: how much extra cost for how much extra health. At district level the same idea is used with ordinary programme units, and the comparison is with other uses of the same NHM money.

NMB AND NHB
ICER is a ratio. Ratios misbehave when extra effect is very small or negative. NMB and NHB turn the same information into a linear score: choose the option with the highest NMB (or NHB) at the stated threshold. They also make it easier to add options and to handle uncertainty.

NMB = (E × k) − C
NHB = E − (C / k)
where k is the threshold.

When extra effect is near zero, the ICER shoots toward infinity and becomes hard to interpret. When extra effect is negative, the sign of the ratio can mislead. Net monetary benefit avoids that problem by giving each option a score: health valued at the threshold, minus cost. Choose the highest score at the stated threshold. Net health benefit is the same ranking, written in health units rather than money.

> **EXAM TIP:** Write ICER formula with C1, C0, E1, E0 named → draw the four-quadrant plane and label dominate / reject / threshold → one sentence that the threshold is health displaced, not a price of life → India: no single statutory k; still use incremental thinking. For a short note, formula plus plane is enough.

LEAGUE TABLES AND ABUSE
A league table of cost per QALY from mixed studies is not a menu. Studies differ in perspective, comparator, time horizon, discount rate, and setting. Transferring a ratio from one country to another without checking prices, practice, and epidemiology is a standard flaw (see the ten-question appraisal checklist).

A published list of "cost per QALY" figures from different papers cannot be used as a shopping list. Each study used its own comparator, prices, time horizon, and discount rate. A ratio from another country cannot be copied into an Indian decision unless those differences have been checked.

KEY POINTS
- Always incremental: extra cost / extra effect.
- Quadrant II: accept. Quadrant IV: reject. I and III: need k.
- Threshold k estimates opportunity cost of displaced care.
- NMB and NHB are linear forms of the same rule.
- India: HTAIn uses cost-effectiveness; there is no single national k to memorise as law.

FORMULAS AND CALCULATIONS
ICER = (C1 − C0) / (E1 − E0)
NMB = E × k − C
