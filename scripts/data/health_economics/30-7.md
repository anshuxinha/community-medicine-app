OVERVIEW OF THE CHAPTER
Health financing is the function of a health system concerned with mobilisation, accumulation, and allocation of money to cover people's health needs. The World Health Organization (WHO) describes three sub-functions: revenue collection, pooling of risks, and purchasing of services. This section is the India paper: sources of funds, National Health Accounts (NHA), out-of-pocket expenditure (OOPE), catastrophic spending, publicly funded insurance, community resources, and a short note on microfinance as it appears in resident teaching.

Evaluation methods tell whether a programme is worth buying. Financing tells who pays and whether payment is pooled.

DEFINITIONS
HEALTH FINANCING: The function of a health system concerned with the mobilisation, accumulation, and allocation of money to cover the health needs of the people. It includes revenue collection, pooling of financial risks, and purchasing of health services.

OUT-OF-POCKET EXPENDITURE (OOPE): Direct payments by households at the point of receiving care that are not reimbursed by any third party. Medicines, tests, consultation fees, and travel paid by the family are typical items.

CATASTROPHIC HEALTH EXPENDITURE: Household OOPE that exceeds a defined share of household resources. Two standard WHO-linked thresholds are used in examinations:
- Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3.8.2: OOPE greater than 10% of total household expenditure or income (a 25% threshold is also reported).
- Capacity-to-pay approach: OOPE greater than 40% of household capacity to pay for health care (expenditure net of subsistence needs such as food, housing, and utilities in the WHO/Europe method).

Do not write "40% of non-health expenditure" as if it were the official phrase. Use capacity to pay.

NATIONAL HEALTH ACCOUNTS (NHA): A systematic description of financial flows in a country's health system, following an international framework. In India the National Health Accounts Technical Secretariat at the National Health Systems Resource Centre (NHSRC), under the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare (MoHFW), produces the estimates.

SOURCES OF FINANCE
Money for health in India comes from four practical streams. A long answer should name each, then say what is pooled and what is not.

1. Government (Union and States). Tax-funded services, national programmes, National Health Mission (NHM), state health departments. Health is a State subject; the Centre steers through policy and centrally sponsored schemes. National Health Policy (NHP), 2017 set a goal of raising government health expenditure toward 2.5% of Gross Domestic Product (GDP) by 2025. That is a policy goal, not an automatic current figure.
2. Households. OOPE at the point of use. This is not pooling. It is the household paying because it has no (or incomplete) third-party cover at that moment.
3. Insurance and social security. Government-funded insurance (notably Ayushman Bharat Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana, AB-PMJAY), Employees' State Insurance, Central Government Health Scheme, state schemes, and private voluntary insurance. Pooling is the economic point: many contribute, the sick draw.
4. External agencies, non-governmental organisations, and corporate social responsibility. World Health Organization, development partners, philanthropic foundations, and NGO service delivery. Useful at the margin and in specific programmes; not the backbone of universal cover.

WHO building-block language calls this "health-care financing." Collection without pooling leaves the sick household exposed. Pooling without strategic purchasing buys volume without value. Economic evaluation is the tool for purchasing.

NHA 2022-23 (OFFICIAL ROUND)
The Union Health Ministry released National Health Accounts Estimates for India 2022-23 in 2026 (tenth NHA report, NHATS/NHSRC). Headline examination figures, each with a year label:

- Out-of-pocket expenditure as a share of Total Health Expenditure declined from 64.2% in 2013-14 to 43.4% in 2022-23.
- Government Health Expenditure was 1.48% of GDP in 2022-23 (new GDP series) and 43.7% of THE.
- For context, 2021-22 (pandemic year) showed a higher government share (GHE about 1.84% of GDP and 48% of THE) and OOPE 39.4% of THE. The 2022-23 round shows government share easing after that surge while remaining well above 2013-14.

NHP-2017's 2.5% of GDP target was not met in the 2022-23 accounts. Write the target as a target and the NHA figure as the account.

Government's share of THE rose over the decade as OOPE's share fell. Households still contribute the largest single slice of current spending. That is the financial-protection gap AB-PMJAY and free-care policies are meant to close.

[SN]Catastrophic health expenditure[/SN]

CATASTROPHIC HEALTH EXPENDITURE
OOPE becomes catastrophic when it forces a household to cut essential consumption or to borrow and sell assets. Measurement uses a threshold, not a clinical judgement.

Use in an answer:
1. Define OOPE.
2. State the 10% (SDG 3.8.2) and 40% of capacity-to-pay thresholds.
3. Link to impoverishing expenditure (OOPE that pushes a household across a poverty line, or that is incurred by a household already poor).
4. India hook: high OOPE, especially on medicines and private outpatient care, is the usual pathway to catastrophe. Inpatient public insurance reduces one part of the bill; it does not automatically cover outpatient drugs.

> **EXAM TIP:** Definition of catastrophic spending → 10% of total household expenditure (SDG) and 40% of capacity to pay → one line on impoverishment → India: OOPE share from NHA 2022-23 with the year. Do not quote a percentage of households as catastrophic unless you are citing a named survey round.

[LAQ]Role of Ayushman Bharat Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana in reducing out-of-pocket expenditure[/LAQ]

AB-PMJAY AND OOPE
Ayushman Bharat has two operational pillars: Health and Wellness Centres (Ayushman Arogya Mandir) for comprehensive primary care, and PM-JAY for secondary and tertiary hospitalisation cover for eligible families. This section is the insurance limb.

How PM-JAY is meant to cut OOPE
- Pools public money to pay for a defined hospitalisation benefit, so the eligible patient is not the first payer at empanelled hospitals.
- Cashless, paperless design at empanelled public and private hospitals, with portability across States in the scheme's architecture.
- Targets families that, without cover, would face large inpatient bills (the classic catastrophic event).
- Complements (does not replace) free care in public facilities and NHM programmes.

Advantages (examination list)
1. Large public pool: risk is not left with the sick household.
2. Inpatient financial protection for eligible admissions, including many procedures that previously produced distress financing.
3. Portability supports migrant workers.
4. Empanelment can bring private capacity under public purchasing.
5. Data on claims can, in principle, support strategic purchasing and fraud control.
6. Pairs with primary-care expansion so that not every need becomes a hospital claim.

Limits and disadvantages (must be in the same answer)
1. OOPE continues for outpatient care, drugs, and diagnostics outside the package, which are a large part of household spending in NHA.
2. Awareness, card/eligibility processes, and denial at the hospital gate still produce unpaid bills.
3. Package rates may not cover high-end private bills; balance billing can persist where rules are weak.
4. Public hospitals need drugs, diagnostics, and staff; insurance without supply-side investment shifts the bill rather than removing it.
5. Moral hazard and supplier-induced demand are financing risks if purchasing is only fee-for-admission.
6. Equity depends on who is eligible and who can actually use empanelled facilities.

Conclusion for the long answer. PM-JAY is a pooling and purchasing reform aimed at inpatient OOPE. NHA shows OOPE's share of THE has fallen over a decade but remains high. A complete answer pairs insurance with primary care, free drugs and diagnostics in public facilities, and the NHP-2017 public-investment goal.

> **EXAM TIP:** Define PM-JAY as the hospitalisation pillar of Ayushman Bharat → six mechanisms for reducing OOPE → six limits (outpatient drugs, awareness, balance billing, supply side, moral hazard, eligibility) → close with NHA 2022-23 OOPE share. Balanced table scores.

COMMUNITY RESOURCES
How can community resources strengthen financing in a State?
- Risk pooling at small scale: community-based health insurance, where it still exists, with a warning that small pools collapse when the sick join and the healthy leave.
- Prepayment into local funds (Rogi Kalyan Samiti / hospital management committees; Village Health Sanitation and Nutrition Committee untied funds) for local gaps, not for replacing tax finance.
- Time and organisation: ASHAs, self-help groups, and village bodies that raise utilisation of already-paid public services (which reduces future OOPE by catching illness earlier).
- Local philanthropy and CSR for specified inputs, with governance so that they fill gaps rather than fragment the system.
- Demand-side: community monitoring of user charges and of PM-JAY empanelment so that entitled care is actually free at the point of use.

Community money cannot replace general taxation for universal coverage. It can reduce leakages and last-mile gaps.

[SN]Microfinance and women empowerment[/SN]

MICROFINANCE AND WOMEN EMPOWERMENT
Microfinance is the provision of small loans and related financial services to people who lack access to conventional banking. In resident teaching it is linked to women's economic and social empowerment, which then affects household health (nutrition, care-seeking, bargaining over expenditure).

Sources: microfinance institutions, self-help groups (SHGs), NGOs.
Economic effects: livelihood, financial independence, poverty alleviation.
Social effects: status, decision-making power, reduced gender disparity.

Challenges: financial literacy, social and cultural barriers, high interest in some products, weak market access, sustainability.
India examples: Self Employed Women's Association (SEWA); Kudumbashree (Kerala); SHGs under the National Rural Livelihoods Mission (NRLM); Pradhan Mantri MUDRA Yojana for small-business credit; Stand Up India for scheduled caste, scheduled tribe, and women entrepreneurs.

This is not a method of economic evaluation. It is a financing-and-determinants short note. Keep it inside one page.

> **EXAM TIP:** Define microfinance → SHG pathway → economic and social limbs → two India names (SEWA or Kudumbashree, NRLM) → two challenges. Do not claim it is a substitute for public health finance.

KEY POINTS
- Collection, pooling, purchasing.
- NHA 2022-23: OOPE 43.4% of THE (from 64.2% in 2013-14); GHE 1.48% of GDP.
- NHP-2017 goal of 2.5% of GDP remains the policy benchmark.
- Catastrophic: 10% of total household spend (SDG) or 40% of capacity to pay.
- PM-JAY pools inpatient risk; outpatient OOPE still matters.
- Community resources strengthen, they do not replace, tax finance.

FORMULAS AND CALCULATIONS
Catastrophic (SDG 3.8.2): OOPE / household total expenditure (or income) > 0.10 (or > 0.25 for the higher cut-off)
Capacity-to-pay rule: OOPE / capacity to pay > 0.40

MNEMONICS
Sources of finance: government taxes, household pockets, insurance pools, and partners (external and NGO).
