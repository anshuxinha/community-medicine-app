OVERVIEW OF THE CHAPTER
Economic evaluation needs a vehicle: a single clinical study (trial or observational set) or a decision-analytic model that brings evidence from several sources. This section covers decision trees, Markov models, sensitivity analysis, and a ten-question checklist for reading a published study. It ends with a worked scaffold for planning an economic evaluation of a programme, including the examination question on the economic impact of a pandemic on the health sector.

DEFINITIONS
DECISION-ANALYTIC MODEL: A quantitative structure that represents alternative courses of action, the chance events that follow, and the costs and consequences of each path, so that expected cost and expected effect can be compared.

DECISION TREE: A model drawn from left to right: decision nodes (squares) where a choice is made, chance nodes (circles) where probabilities apply, and terminal nodes where costs and effects are attached. Folding back (expected value at each chance node) yields the expected cost and effect of each decision.

MARKOV MODEL: A model for problems that unfold over repeated cycles. Individuals occupy mutually exclusive health states (for example well, sick, dead). Each cycle they may stay or move according to transition probabilities. Useful when events can recur (hospitalisation, relapse) or when timing stretches beyond a one-off tree.

SENSITIVITY ANALYSIS: A method to assess how changes in input variables affect the results of a model. One-at-a-time analysis varies one parameter while holding others. Global analysis varies many parameters together. Probabilistic sensitivity analysis (often implemented as Monte Carlo simulation) draws parameters from distributions and reports the distribution of results.

CHEERS: Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards. A reporting guideline for published evaluations. The ten-question checklist below is the appraisal tool to use in an examination answer.

DECISION TREES
A tree forces the analyst to name alternatives and pathways. Example (cancer chemotherapy versus supportive care).

Decision: chemotherapy or no chemotherapy.
Chance after chemotherapy: neutropenia with fever (probability p1) or not (1 − p1).
Given fever: hospitalisation, outpatient treatment, or no further treatment, each with a probability and a cost.
Chance after no chemotherapy: fever from disease (p2) or not.

Each terminal node has a cost and an effect (survival, QALYs, or a clinical endpoint). Expected cost of a branch = sum of (probability of path × cost of path). Expected effect is computed the same way. Compare expected costs and effects incrementally.

Trees become bushy when events repeat or when time is long. Then a Markov structure is cleaner: states, cycles (monthly, yearly), transitions, and a time horizon long enough to capture major costs and effects (often lifetime for mortality-changing interventions).

> **EXAM TIP:** Define tree → square / circle / triangle (or terminal) → one drawn example with two branches and probabilities that add to 1 → expected value in one line. Say when to switch to Markov (recurrence, long horizon).

SENSITIVITY ANALYSIS
Point estimates pretend to be certain. They are not. Characterise uncertainty.

1. One-at-a-time (OAT): vary the discount rate, a key probability, a unit cost, or a utility between a low and a high plausible value. Easy to report. Misses interactions.
2. Global: move several parameters at once (for example a worst-case and best-case scenario).
3. Probabilistic (Monte Carlo): each uncertain parameter has a distribution. Many random draws produce a cloud of ICERs on the cost-effectiveness plane and a CEAC.

Also report: statistical uncertainty when patient-level cost and effect data exist; heterogeneity (results for relevant subgroups, not only the average patient); structural uncertainty (would a different model change the decision?).

If conclusions flip when a plausible input changes, do not treat the base case as a fact. If they do not flip, the decision is robust to that input.

> **EXAM TIP:** Define → OAT versus global versus probabilistic in a three-row table → "if the decision flips, say so." Do not write code.

TEN QUESTIONS FOR A SOUND EVALUATION
Use this list to appraise a paper and to plan a study. It is the examination skeleton.

1. Was a well-defined question posed in answerable form? Both costs and effects; comparison of alternatives; perspective stated; population and subgroups defined; time horizon appropriate.
2. Was a comprehensive description of the competing alternatives given? Who did what to whom, where, and how often? Was doing nothing considered? Were relevant alternatives omitted?
3. Was the effectiveness of the programmes established? Trial, systematic review, or observational data, with bias discussed. Economic evaluation cannot create effectiveness by itself.
4. Were all important and relevant costs and consequences identified? Range wide enough; capital and operating costs; perspectives covered.
5. Were costs and consequences measured accurately in appropriate physical units before valuation? Sources of utilisation; joint costs handled; omitted items justified.
6. Were costs and consequences valued credibly? Sources of prices and of preference weights named; market distortions adjusted if a societal valuation is claimed.
7. Were costs and consequences adjusted for differential timing? Discounted to present value; rate justified.
8. Was an incremental analysis performed? Extra cost compared with extra effect, not average ratios only.
9. Was uncertainty adequately characterised? Statistics, sensitivity analysis, subgroups.
10. Did presentation and discussion cover issues of concern to users? Intelligent use of ratios; comparison with other studies; generalisability; equity and ethics; implementation and freed resources; implications of uncertainty for further research.

**Mnemonic:** QUEST VALUE

QUEST (the question and the facts):
- Q: Question answerable (alternatives, perspective, population)
- U: Unbiased description of options (who does what to whom)
- E: Effectiveness established (not assumed)
- S: Scope of costs and consequences identified
- T: Tallied in physical units before money

VALUE (the valuation and the decision):
- V: Valued credibly
- A: Adjusted for timing (discount)
- L: Listed incrementally (ICER, not only averages)
- U: Uncertainty characterised
- E: Explained for users (generalisability, equity, implementation)

The ten questions map onto two words: QUEST and VALUE.

PLANNING AN EVALUATION (GENERAL)
1. Decision problem and perspective.
2. Population and subgroups.
3. Alternatives, including current practice and, if relevant, doing nothing.
4. Time horizon long enough for major intended and unintended effects.
5. Effectiveness evidence (trial or synthesis).
6. Costs: identify, measure, value (health sector, patient, others as per perspective).
7. Consequences: natural unit, QALY, or money, matching the method.
8. Discounting.
9. Model or trial-based analysis.
10. Uncertainty and subgroups.
11. Presentation: incremental results, plane, NMB/NHB if a threshold is used, limits, implementation.

PANDEMIC IMPACT ON THE HEALTH SECTOR (WORKED LAQ)
Question: plan and conduct an economic evaluation of the impact of a pandemic (for example coronavirus disease 2019) on the health sector.

This is not only a cost-of-illness total. A full evaluation still needs alternatives: for example "response as implemented" versus "no additional response," or versus an alternative intensity (earlier non-pharmaceutical measures, different testing strategy, different vaccination timing). If the paper also asks for public-health lessons, keep those clinical and health-system points separate from the economics limb.

Perspective. Health system for hospital and programme costs; societal if household OOPE, deaths, long disability, and lost production are in scope. State which and why.

Time horizon. At least the pandemic waves plus a tail for long complications, postponed elective care, and vaccination programme costs. Discount if the tail exceeds one year.

Costs to identify.
- Direct health-sector: testing, personal protective equipment, intensive care, oxygen, vaccines, staff overtime, new isolation wards, information systems.
- Displaced care: cancelled elective surgery and missed chronic-disease follow-up (opportunity cost inside the system).
- Patient and family: OOPE, travel, isolation time.
- Other sectors only if the perspective includes them.

Consequences.
- Deaths averted, hospitalisations averted, QALYs or DALYs, and, if CBA is attempted, a money value. Do not invent a national QALY total. Structure the calculation.

Vehicle. A model is almost always required: decision tree for acute pathways (home, ward, intensive care, death); Markov or multi-state model for longer disability; possibly a transmission model if the intervention changes infection rates. Trial-based evaluation of a whole national response is not available in real time.

Uncertainty. Test higher and lower transmission, vaccine effect, unit costs of intensive care, and discount rate. Report what would flip a conclusion that "response X was cost-effective."

Presentation. Incremental cost per death averted or per QALY for specified strategies; a CCA table if too many outcomes refuse to fit one unit; discussion of equity (who bore OOPE and who was displaced) and of implementation (surge capacity).

> **EXAM TIP:** For a methods long answer: ten-point QUEST VALUE checklist. For the pandemic question: alternatives and perspective first (or it is only a bill), then cost categories including displaced care, then a model, then uncertainty. Do not write only lessons from lockdowns.

KEY POINTS
- Trees for one-off pathways; Markov for repeating risk and time.
- Sensitivity analysis is part of the result, not an optional annex.
- Ten-question checklist is the appraisal and the protocol.
- CHEERS is the reporting standard; the checklist is what to memorise for theory.
- A pandemic "impact" study is an evaluation only if alternatives are compared.
