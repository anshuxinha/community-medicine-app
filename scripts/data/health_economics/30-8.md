OVERVIEW OF THE CHAPTER
Earlier sections answered how to compare programmes. This section answers how those comparisons are used: to allocate resources, to move toward Universal Health Coverage (UHC), and to strengthen the building blocks of the health system. A medical officer who can compute an ICER but cannot say what to do with a district budget has not finished the paper.

DEFINITIONS
UNIVERSAL HEALTH COVERAGE (UHC): All people have access to the health services they need, when and where they need them, without financial hardship. WHO describes UHC along three dimensions often drawn as a cube: who is covered (population), which services are covered (service package), and what share of cost is covered (financial protection). Expanding UHC means extending one or more of those three edges toward the full cube.

WHO HEALTH SYSTEM BUILDING BLOCKS: Six core components: service delivery; health workforce; health information system; access to essential medicines; financing; leadership and governance. Community participation is taught in Indian notes as a seventh, cross-cutting requirement so that the blocks serve people rather than only facilities.

PRIMARY HEALTH CARE (PHC), FOUR A's: Care that is Affordable, Acceptable, Available, and Accessible. Alma-Ata also requires that methods be scientifically sound and socially acceptable, and that communities participate. The four A's are the usual short-note list.

RESOURCE ALLOCATION: The decision to put limited inputs (staff, money, drugs, time) into one use rather than another. In public health it should follow need, effectiveness, cost-effectiveness, equity, and feasibility, not only historical budgets.

THE UHC CUBE
Draw a cube. Three axes:

1. Population covered: from the poorest or currently entitled groups toward the whole population.
2. Services covered: from a narrow package (safe delivery, immunisation, a short list of admissions) toward a comprehensive set including non-communicable disease, mental health, rehabilitation.
3. Cost covered: from high OOPE toward prepayment and pooling so that the user fee at the point of care falls.

India's direction of travel in policy: NHP-2017; comprehensive primary care through Ayushman Arogya Mandir; AB-PMJAY for hospitalisation of eligible families; free drugs and diagnostics in public facilities as a financial-protection tool. NHA 2022-23 still shows OOPE at 43.4% of THE, so the "cost covered" axis is incomplete. Evaluation (this chapter) is how to choose which services to add when the cube cannot grow on all three axes at once.

> **EXAM TIP:** Define UHC in WHO's one sentence → draw the cube and name the three axes → one India line per axis (entitlement, AAM package, PM-JAY plus remaining OOPE). Close: "raise all three edges; do not confuse insurance enrolment with UHC."

BUILDING BLOCKS
Write them as a table, not a paragraph.

| Block | What it is | Allocation question |
|---|---|---|
| Service delivery | Safe, quality care at the right level | Which package at sub-centre, PHC, CHC, district hospital? |
| Health workforce | Numbers, skill mix, distribution, motivation | Where to post the next medical officer or CHO? |
| Information | HMIS, surveillance, NHA, claims data | Which indicator will change a monthly review? |
| Essential medicines | Selection, procurement, supply, quality | ABC-VED and EOQ belong here as store tools |
| Financing | Collection, pooling, purchasing | Tax, insurance, OOPE mix; what to buy |
| Leadership / governance | Policy, regulation, accountability | Who decides, with what evidence (HTAIn, DHS)? |

Community participation sits across the table: VHSNC, Jan Arogya Samiti, patient groups. Without it, delivery and financing reforms do not reach the people whose opportunity cost is being decided.

Services and workforce need information, medicines, leadership, and financing. Community participation holds the blocks together.

> **EXAM TIP:** List six blocks with one allocation question each → add community participation as the taught seventh → one line linking financing to the evaluation methods in this chapter.

PHC AND THE FOUR A's
Affordable: cost to the user does not deter needed care (pooling, free drugs, PM-JAY).
Acceptable: culture, language, gender, opening hours, trust.
Available: the service exists (staff, drugs, hours).
Accessible: geography, disability, and social barriers do not block use.

The four A's are a named set. Examinations often pair them with the UHC cube: the A's describe the service; the cube describes coverage at population scale.

[SN]Health Technology Assessment in India[/SN]

HTAIn AND ALLOCATION
Health Technology Assessment in India (HTAIn), Department of Health Research, collates and generates evidence on clinical effectiveness, cost-effectiveness, and safety of medicines, devices, and programmes. That evidence is an input to allocation at national and state level: which device to list, which programme to scale, which medicine to put on the National List of Essential Medicines conversation.

A district medical officer will rarely run a full CUA. The same logic still applies in miniature:
- Name alternatives (including current practice).
- Count extra cost and extra effect.
- Ask what will be displaced (opportunity cost).
- Check equity: who gains, who pays remaining OOPE.
- Record the decision so it can be reviewed.

A medical officer should be able to advise on allocation of resources in the work area. That advice is this checklist, not a private preference.

USING EVALUATION IN A DISTRICT (LAQ CLOSE)
A one-year district plan that uses this chapter would:

1. Situation analysis (burden, coverage, OOPE distress, workforce).
2. Short list of feasible alternatives for the top problems.
3. For each shortlisted pair, a CEA in natural units that the district can measure (extra fully immunised child, extra controlled hypertensive, extra sputum-positive treated).
4. Where two programmes compete for the same untied fund, prefer the option with more health per extra rupee unless an equity override is stated (for example a small tribal population with catastrophic transport costs).
5. Protect vital and high-value commodities (ABC-VED) so that "savings" do not empty the emergency tray.
6. Monitor with HMIS; evaluate; reallocate.

This is extra-welfarist practice in the field: the objective is health (and financial protection), the constraint is the budget, and the method is incremental comparison.

CONCLUSION
Health economics is the study of how scarce resources are allocated to produce, distribute, and consume health and health care. Choices will be made on other people's behalf. Economic evaluation is the comparative analysis of alternative courses of action in terms of both their costs and consequences. Opportunity cost is the health (or other valued benefit) given up. India still pays a large share of THE out of pocket. Expanding the UHC cube, funding the building blocks, and using HTA and simple incremental arithmetic at district level are the same job at three scales.

> **EXAM TIP:** LAQ frame: definition of allocation → criteria (need, effect, cost-effectiveness, equity, feasibility) → UHC cube → six blocks → one district worked example → close with opportunity cost. For a short note, cube or blocks, not both in full.

KEY POINTS
- UHC cube: population, services, cost share.
- Six WHO blocks plus community participation.
- Four A's of PHC.
- HTAIn supplies national evidence; districts still use incremental logic.
- Allocation without naming what is displaced is not economics.
