OVERVIEW OF THE CHAPTER
The basic tasks of economic evaluation are to identify, measure, value, and compare. This leaf is the cost side of that sequence. Consequences are valued in the next leaf. Discounting, which applies to both costs and health effects that fall in different years, is written here because examinations usually attach it to costing.

A cost is a resource used up because a programme exists, valued so that it can be compared with another use of the same resource. The rupee figure on a budget line is a starting point, not automatically the opportunity cost.

DEFINITIONS
COST (IN ECONOMIC EVALUATION): The value of resources consumed by a programme, measured so as to approximate what is given up (opportunity cost). Identification lists the ingredients. Measurement records physical units (hours, bed-days, doses). Valuation attaches a rupee (or other currency) amount, usually from market prices, with adjustment when the market price is missing or distorted.

PERSPECTIVE: The viewpoint from which costs (and consequences) are counted. A health-system perspective includes health-sector costs. A societal perspective includes health-sector costs, other-sector costs, patient and family costs, and, where the protocol requires it, productivity effects.

MARGINAL COST: The extra cost of producing one more unit of a product or service. In symbols, marginal cost = change in total cost / change in quantity.

In epidemiology the nearest cousin is the number needed to treat: extra effort for one extra success. Marginal cost asks the same question in money.

DISCOUNTING: A mathematical procedure that converts future costs and consequences into present values so that streams with different time profiles can be compared. Present value of an amount arising n years from now at annual discount rate r is 1 / (1 + r)^n times that amount.

CAPITAL COST: The cost of assets that last beyond one accounting year (land, buildings, equipment, vehicles). In evaluation they are usually annuitized over useful life rather than entered as a single purchase spike.

RECURRENT (OPERATING) COST: Costs that are used up within the year (staff salaries, drugs, electricity, fuel, maintenance, consumables).

[LAQ]Cost analysis in a health programme[/LAQ]

IDENTIFY, MEASURE, VALUE
Four cost categories are enough for an examination answer.

1. Health-sector resources: staff time, drugs, diagnostics, bed-days, overheads (power, cleaning, administration), capital. Include the cost of managing adverse events caused by the programme.
2. Patient and family resources: out-of-pocket payments (OOPE), travel, time seeking care, informal care.
3. Other sectors: social services, education, voluntary organisations, criminal justice, as relevant (common in mental health, elderly care, and some public-health laws).
4. Productivity: time off work. Whether to include it is disputed. Extra-welfarist analyses that already value health as QALYs often omit a separate wage bill to avoid double counting. If it is included, the health-state description used for preference scores should not already contain lost income.

Joint costs (hospital overheads shared by many services) must be allocated on a stated basis (floor area, staff numbers, cases). Omitting overhead because "a small change will not affect the power bill" is a classic error: applied to every service, the hospital would run without light.

Unthinking use of an average hospital per-diem for a specific condition is another classic error. Hotel costs may be close to the average; medical treatment costs usually are not.

PROVIDER COST AND PURCHASER COST
Resident notes split costs as the provider sees them and as the purchaser (patient, insurer, government) sees them. The split is useful in India, where the same episode can look cheap to a public hospital and expensive to a household.

| | Provider (facility or programme) | Purchaser (patient, insurer, government) |
|---|---|---|
| Capital | Land, building, equipment, vehicle | Rarely visible to the patient except as fees set to recover capital |
| Recurrent | Staff, electricity, fuel, supplies (often the bulk of a facility budget) | Premiums, taxes, user fees |
| Direct money at the point of use | Budget lines | OOPE: medicines, tests, travel, informal payments |
| Indirect / time | Overtime, diversion of staff | Loss of work time and pay; caregiver time |

Direct OOPE is a purchaser cost. It is also a patient-and-family cost in the four-category scheme above. Do not count it twice.

NATIONAL CONTEXT
National Health Accounts (NHA) estimates for India, 2022-23 (MoHFW / National Health Accounts Technical Secretariat): out-of-pocket expenditure was 43.4% of Total Health Expenditure (THE), down from 64.2% in 2013-14. Government Health Expenditure was 1.48% of Gross Domestic Product (GDP) and 43.7% of THE. Recurrent staff and commodity costs dominate public facility budgets; households still pay a large share at the point of use, especially for medicines.

[SN]Marginal cost[/SN]

MARGINAL COST
Average cost is total cost divided by quantity. Marginal cost is the extra cost of one more unit. They coincide only under constant returns.

Why it matters in health: adding the tenth bed, the next outreach session, or the next laboratory test is not always as cheap as the average of what has already been done. Overheads are spread at first (average cost falls), then congestion and overtime appear (marginal cost rises). Decisions to expand should look at marginal, not only average, cost.

Example. A cold-chain point already staffed can add a session at the cost of vaccines, fuel, and a few hours. Opening a new point in an unserved hamlet adds capital, training, and a new session plan. The average cost of "all sessions in the block" hides that difference.

> **EXAM TIP:** Write the definition (extra cost of one more unit) → contrast with average cost → one health example (extra session versus new site) → one line that expansion decisions use marginal cost.

[SN]Discounting[/SN]

DISCOUNTING
Costs and health effects that occur in different years must be brought to one date, usually the present. A hypertension programme spends now and prevents strokes later. An influenza campaign spends and benefits in the same season. Without discounting, the delayed programme is favoured only because its costs look "later," which is not a reason.

The procedure is present value. If r is the annual discount rate and n is the number of years until the cost or effect occurs:

Present value = future amount × 1 / (1 + r)^n

A higher discount rate, or a longer delay, lowers present value. Discount tables exist; a resident needs the idea and the formula, not a memorised table.

Why discount at all? Time preference: as individuals and as a society we prefer resources now rather than later, because they can be used in the interim. Resources committed today could be invested and would generate more resources (and potentially more health) later. Time preference is not only a financial idea, so health effects that occur later are also discounted in standard analyses. Equal rates for costs and health effects are the usual exam default unless the question specifies a published exception.

Choose a rate and justify it. Many published studies use 3% or 5% per year. State the rate, discount both sides, and test other rates in sensitivity analysis.

> **EXAM TIP:** Definition → formula → "higher r or larger n lowers present value" → discount both costs and health effects at the same stated rate → one sentence on time preference. Skip a lecture on whose rate is philosophically correct.

[SN]Economic order quantity[/SN]

ECONOMIC ORDER QUANTITY (EOQ)
EOQ is a production-scheduling model. It is the order size that meets demand while minimising the sum of ordering costs and holding (storage) costs.

EOQ = square root of (2 × S × D / H)
- S: cost per order (setup, shipping, handling)
- D: demand (quantity used per year)
- H: holding cost per unit per year

The graph is a U-shaped total-cost curve. Ordering cost falls as order size rises (fewer orders). Holding cost rises as order size rises (more stock). EOQ sits at the minimum of the sum. A related store diagram shows inventory falling with use, a reorder point, lead time, and receipt of the new order.

Use in health: drug and vaccine stores, not as a method of economic evaluation of programmes. It sits next to Always Better Control (ABC) and Vital-Essential-Desirable (VED) analysis taught under inventory control. EOQ assumes reasonably steady demand and stable order and holding costs. Real stores face outbreaks, seasonal demand, and cold-chain limits, so the formula is a starting point, not a law.

Helps to avoid over-ordering and under-ordering, to cut storage cost, and to improve fulfilment. Limitation: a static formula under changing demand.

> **EXAM TIP:** One-line definition → formula with S, D, H named → U-shaped total cost → one hospital-store sentence → one limitation. Do not turn this into a full evaluation essay.

PRODUCTIVITY AND TIME
Patient time in treatment is a cost. Healthy time created by a programme is a consequence. Counting wages as a cost and again inside a QALY is double counting if the preference score already reflects being able to work. State a rule and keep it.

KEY POINTS
- Identify, measure, value, compare.
- Four buckets: health sector, patient/family, other sectors, productivity (with a double-counting warning).
- Capital versus recurrent; provider versus purchaser; direct OOPE versus time.
- Marginal cost is the extra cost of one more unit.
- Discount future amounts: 1/(1+r)^n.
- EOQ is inventory mathematics, not a type of programme evaluation.

FORMULAS AND CALCULATIONS
Marginal cost = ΔC / ΔQ
Present value = future amount × 1 / (1 + r)^n
EOQ = √(2SD / H)
