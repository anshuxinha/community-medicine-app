OVERVIEW OF THE CHAPTER
Costs are identified and valued in money in much the same way across study types. What distinguishes one form of economic evaluation from another is how consequences are measured and valued. The methods table a resident should be able to draw from memory is: cost analysis (not a full evaluation), cost-minimization analysis (CMA), cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA), cost-utility analysis (CUA), cost-benefit analysis (CBA), and cost-consequences analysis (CCA).

Costing, valuing health, and decision rules are treated in more depth later in this chapter. Here the job is classification, when each method is useful, and what it cannot do.

DEFINITIONS
COST-EFFECTIVENESS ANALYSIS (CEA): An economic evaluation in which costs are measured in monetary units and consequences are measured as a single effect of interest, common to both alternatives, in natural units (for example life-years gained, cases detected, millimetres of mercury of blood-pressure reduction, episode-free days). Results are usually expressed as incremental cost per unit of effect.

COST-UTILITY ANALYSIS (CUA): A form of cost-effectiveness analysis that uses a generic measure of health gain, typically the quality-adjusted life-year (QALY), so that programmes in different disease areas can be compared. Results are usually expressed as incremental cost per QALY gained. "Utility" here means a preference score for a health state, not a casual synonym for usefulness.

COST-BENEFIT ANALYSIS (CBA): An economic evaluation in which both costs and consequences are valued in monetary units, so that a net benefit (or a ratio of benefits to costs) can be stated. Monetary valuation of health effects commonly uses willingness to pay (WTP).

COST-MINIMIZATION ANALYSIS (CMA): A comparison of costs alone, used only when consequences of the alternatives can reasonably be treated as equivalent. It is not a unique study design that can be declared in advance of seeing the results.

COST-CONSEQUENCES ANALYSIS (CCA): A comparison that arrays costs and a set of consequences in their natural units without combining them into a single ratio. The decision-maker sees the profile and applies weights.

COST ANALYSIS: Measurement of the costs of one or more options without a full comparison of consequences. Cost-of-illness (burden of illness) studies describe the cost of a disease to society. They are not full economic evaluations because alternatives are not compared on both costs and consequences.

THE MEASUREMENT TABLE
The identification and monetary valuation of costs is similar across evaluations. The nature of consequences is not.

| Type of study | Costs | Consequences identified | How consequences are valued |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cost analysis / cost of illness | Money | None compared across alternatives | Not a full evaluation |
| CMA | Money | Effects treated as equivalent | Comparison reduces to cost |
| CEA | Money | Single effect common to both alternatives, different in size | Natural units (life-years, cases, mmHg, days) |
| CUA | Money | Single or multiple effects, not necessarily the same events | Healthy years, typically QALYs |
| CBA | Money | Single or multiple effects, not necessarily common | Money (WTP or market values) |
| CCA | Money | Several effects listed | Left in natural units; no single index |

WHEN EACH METHOD HELPS
CEA is most useful when a decision-maker with a given budget is comparing a limited set of options that share one effect: a hypertension programme judged by millimetres of mercury reduced; a screening programme judged by cases detected. The largest limit is that a disease-specific effect does not show what is given up in other programmes funded from the same budget. A generic measure is needed before opportunity cost across the whole budget can be judged.

CUA is the form now most often published and most often requested by health-technology bodies, because the QALY (or a similar generic unit) lets a cancer drug, a cardiac procedure, and a mental-health programme be placed on one scale. Disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) are a related generic health-gap measure used in Global Burden of Disease work and in World Health Organization (WHO) CHOICE analyses. They are not a third, separate "best for the health system" method. They are an alternative generic denominator. QALYs measure health gained. DALYs measure health lost. Both are generic units. Neither is a separate method of evaluation.

CBA can, in principle, ask a wider question: is it worth expanding the health budget at all, because benefits are in the same unit as costs and can be compared with non-health uses of public money. In practice, full CBAs that place a money value on all health effects are uncommon. WTP studies exist; few are assembled into a complete CBA of a programme.

CMA looks tidy when two options "do the same thing." Because of statistical uncertainty around both costs and effects, results rarely sit in a box labelled "equal effect." CMA is justifiable mainly when the technologies are near-identical (two drugs of the same class with established equivalence). It should not be chosen as a design on the protocol's first page without that prior evidence.

CCA is honest when several outcomes matter and forcing them into one number would hide important differences (convenience, equity, waiting time, as well as health). The cost is that the reader must supply the weights.

**Mnemonic:** Equal, Effect, Utility, Benefit
- Equal effects: minimise cost (CMA)
- Effect in natural units (CEA)
- Utility as a QALY (CUA)
- Benefit in money (CBA)

LIMITS AND VALUE JUDGEMENTS
The more important differences among methods are not the units on the page. They are the value judgements each method implies.

- CEA and CUA, used with a budget constraint, ask how to maximise health (or a specified effect) from resources already allocated to health. Opportunity cost is health forgone in displaced programmes.
- CBA, when it uses individual WTP, asks a welfare question that can reach beyond the health budget. Whose willingness to pay counts, and whether ability to pay should influence social choice, then become explicit value problems.
- Calling a paper "CEA" or "CBA" is not a reliable guide to which value stance the authors actually took. Read what was measured.

INDIA / PROGRAMME HOOK
A district medical officer comparing two strategies to raise full immunisation coverage (fixed sessions versus additional mobile teams) can often use CEA: cost per extra fully immunised child, or cost per extra measles case averted if surveillance is good. A state considering a new cancer medicine against a cardiac intervention from the same budget needs a generic measure (CUA with QALYs, or a DALY-based analysis if that is the evidence to hand). National Health Accounts and insurance design are financing questions; they use these tools but are not themselves a type of evaluation.

HTAIn assessments typically need clinical effect plus cost-effectiveness. That pairing is CEA or CUA, not a cost-of-illness pamphlet.

WORKED CONTRAST (EXAM SIZE)
Question: should a block add a second weekly Village Health and Nutrition Day (VHND) in hard-to-reach hamlets?

- Cost analysis: extra ANM travel and vaccine wastage this year. Incomplete.
- CMA: only if coverage and disease outcomes are already known to be identical, which they will not be if the point of the extra day is to raise coverage.
- CEA: extra cost per additional child fully immunised, or per additional complete antenatal contact.
- CUA: extra cost per QALY if deaths and disability averted can be modelled.
- CBA: money value of averted illness plus time saved, minus extra cost. Hard to do well at block level.
- CCA: cost, coverage, dropout, caregiver time, and staff overtime listed side by side.

Choose CEA for the operational decision. Reserve CUA when the comparison crosses programmes.

KEY POINTS
- Full evaluation: alternatives, costs, and consequences.
- CEA: money / natural unit of one effect.
- CUA: money / generic health (usually QALY).
- CBA: money / money.
- CMA: costs only when effects are equivalent; not a design to announce in advance.
- Cost-of-illness is not economic evaluation.
- DALY is a generic health-gap unit, not a separate method that "beats" CUA.

FORMULAS AND CALCULATIONS
Average cost-effectiveness ratio = cost of A / effect of A. This is not the decision rule. Use the incremental ratio: (cost of A minus cost of B) / (effect of A minus effect of B).

MNEMONICS
Equal, Effect, Utility, Benefit: CMA, CEA, CUA, CBA.
