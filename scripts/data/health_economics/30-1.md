OVERVIEW OF THE CHAPTER
Those who plan, provide, receive, or pay for health services face recurring questions: who should do what, to whom, with what resources, and with what relation to other services. Resources (people, time, facilities, equipment, knowledge, and money) are scarce. Choices will be made whether or not they are based on evidence. Health economics is the study of how scarce resources are allocated to produce, distribute, and consume health and health care. Economic evaluation is the method that makes those choices explicit by comparing both costs and consequences.

This section states the definitions a resident can copy into an examination answer, explains why organised analysis is needed, names the two features of any full economic evaluation, and places Health Technology Assessment (HTA) and the special features of the health market next to those definitions. Methods of analysis (cost-effectiveness, cost-utility, cost-benefit) follow under types of economic evaluation.

DEFINITIONS
HEALTH ECONOMICS: Health economics is the study of how scarce resources are allocated to produce, distribute, and consume health and health care.

In plain language: wants in health always exceed the budget. Every rupee, nurse-hour, or hospital bed used for one patient or programme is unavailable for another. Health economics organises that fact so that a choice can be defended.

ECONOMIC EVALUATION: The comparative analysis of alternative courses of action in terms of both their costs and consequences.

The basic tasks of any economic evaluation are to identify, measure, value, and compare the costs and consequences of the alternatives being considered. A study that lists only costs, or that describes the burden of a disease without a comparator, is not a full economic evaluation.

OPPORTUNITY COST: The real cost of any programme is not the rupees appearing on the programme budget, but the value of the benefits achievable in some other programme that has been forgone by committing those resources to the first programme.

A rupee spent on A is a rupee denied to B. If a district buys a new diagnostic machine, the opportunity cost may be the outreach sessions, drugs, or staff that will not be funded. Economic evaluation exists to estimate that forgone benefit and to set it beside the benefit of the chosen course.

HEALTH TECHNOLOGY ASSESSMENT (HTA): A multidisciplinary process that uses explicit methods to determine the value of a health technology at different points in its lifecycle. Value includes clinical effectiveness, safety, costs, and, where relevant, organisational, social, and ethical effects.

The Government of India created Health Technology Assessment in India (HTAIn) under the Department of Health Research (DHR), Ministry of Health and Family Welfare (MoHFW). HTAIn collates and, where needed, generates evidence on the clinical effectiveness, cost-effectiveness, and safety of medicines, devices, and health programmes.

NET HEALTH BENEFIT: Health gained from a programme minus the health that is given up elsewhere because extra resources were used (the health opportunity cost).

HEALTH ECONOMICS: SCOPE
Health economics is not hospital book-keeping. It sits at the point where clinical evidence meets a budget constraint. Its questions include:

1. What is the purpose of the health system (health itself, broader welfare, or a mix)?
2. Which effects should count, and how should they be measured and valued?
3. What is given up when a new service is adopted (opportunity cost)?
4. From whose viewpoint are costs and consequences counted (perspective)?
5. How should scarce public money, insurance pools, and household payments be organised (financing, treated later in this chapter)?

Economic evaluation is one tool inside that field. Clinical evaluation and economic evaluation are complements, not rivals. Clinical studies estimate effects. Economic evaluation provides a framework to use those effects, together with costs and other valued consequences, when a choice among alternatives cannot be avoided.

TWO FEATURES OF ECONOMIC EVALUATION
Whatever the setting, a full economic evaluation has two features.

1. It deals with both inputs and outputs (costs and consequences). Few people would buy a sealed package without knowing either the price or the contents. Linking what must be given up with what is expected in return is what allows a decision.
2. It concerns itself with choices. Resources are limited. Not every efficacious therapy can be produced. Criteria for choice are often implicit. Economic evaluation makes the criteria explicit, especially when costs fall on some people and consequences accrue to others.

These two features lead back to the definition: comparative analysis of alternative courses of action in terms of both their costs and consequences. The usual form is incremental: the difference in costs is compared with the difference in consequences.

COMPARATIVE ANALYSIS (THE BASIC FIGURE)
An evaluation is formulated as a choice. Programme A (the programme of interest) is compared with Programme B (the comparator). The comparator need not be an active treatment. It may be current practice, a low-cost option, or doing nothing. If the comparator itself is inefficient, a new programme can look attractive only because the baseline is poor. All relevant alternatives should be considered. When only one comparator can be studied, it should be the strategy that best represents current practice in the jurisdiction.

[SN]Why economic evaluation is important[/SN]

WHY ECONOMIC EVALUATION IS IMPORTANT
Organised consideration of costs and consequences is needed for at least four reasons.

1. Alternatives. Without systematic analysis it is hard even to name the relevant options. Introducing a special rehabilitation centre without describing existing episodic care, or without asking whether prevention would achieve the same objective at lower cost, is a common failure.
2. Perspective. A programme that looks unattractive from one viewpoint may look better from another. Perspectives include the patient, the facility, the programme budget, the Ministry of Health, the government as a whole, and society (all costs and consequences to whomsoever they accrue).
3. Quantification. Informal guesses about orders of magnitude mislead. Extra tests, extra visits, or extra days in intensive care can drive cost per extra case detected to extremes that no one would have guessed without measurement.
4. Accountability. Evaluation makes scientific judgements and social value judgements explicit so they can be scrutinised. The main contribution may not be a different decision, but a decision that can be explained.

**Mnemonic:** PACT
- P: Perspective stated
- A: Alternatives named (including a fair baseline)
- C: Counted magnitudes (costs and consequences measured, not guessed)
- T: Transparent accountability (facts separated from values)

A pact is an explicit agreement. Evaluation turns an implicit bargain over other people's health into a statement that can be examined.

> **EXAM TIP:** Open with the definition (scarce resources allocated to produce, distribute, and consume health and health care). Then economic evaluation as comparative analysis of alternatives in costs and consequences. Then opportunity cost in one line. Then PACT as four reasons. Close: "choices will be made; evaluation makes them defensible."

[SN]Health technology assessment[/SN]

HEALTH TECHNOLOGY ASSESSMENT
HTA asks whether a medicine, device, procedure, or programme is worth adopting, continuing, or discontinuing, given safety, effectiveness, and opportunity cost. It is multidisciplinary. Clinical epidemiology, economics, ethics, and organisational analysis sit in the same file.

India. HTAIn, under DHR, MoHFW, is the national institutional arrangement. It works through a secretariat, resource hubs, resource centres, and technical partners. Completed assessments inform public programmes and purchasing. There is no single statutory cost-per-QALY threshold of the kind used by the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) in England. Incremental thinking still applies: extra cost is judged against extra health, and against what else the same money could buy in the Indian public system.

Use of HTA is easier in single-payer or heavily pooled systems, where one authority reimburses on behalf of a population. In multi-payer systems with large household payments, decisions are more dispersed. India has both: tax-funded services and insurance schemes alongside high out-of-pocket spending. That mix is why a national HTA body and a district medical officer both need the same method, at different scales.

> **EXAM TIP:** Define HTA → name HTAIn (DHR) → three products of an assessment (safety, clinical effect, cost-effectiveness) → one line that India has no single published threshold. Do not write a history of NICE.

[SN]Health market characteristics[/SN]

HEALTH MARKET CHARACTERISTICS
In most markets the person who receives the benefit also pays the price and can judge quality. Health care is different. Patients often cannot know what they need, especially at the moment of illness. They rely on a clinician as an agent. That agency relationship is imperfect when information is unequal or when the agent's income depends on the volume of care. Five features recur in examinations.

1. Uncertainty: illness is unpredictable; the product (future health) cannot be inspected before purchase in the way a bicycle can.
2. Asymmetric information: the clinician knows more than the patient about diagnosis, options, and likely effect.
3. Externalities: vaccination, sanitation, and treatment of infectious disease benefit people who did not buy the service.
4. Supplier-induced demand: the agent who advises also supplies; volume can expand beyond need.
5. Third-party payment: government, social insurance, or private insurance pays on the patient's behalf, weakening the usual price signal and creating moral hazard (using more care because someone else pays).

**Mnemonic:** CAUSE
- C: Coverage by a third party (government or insurance pays)
- A: Asymmetric information
- U: Uncertainty of illness and of outcome
- S: Supplier-induced demand
- E: Externalities

These are the causes of market failure in health. They explain why leaving allocation only to an unregulated market does not produce an efficient or fair pattern of care, and why public finance, regulation, and evaluation are used together.

India hook. High out-of-pocket payment does not convert health into an ordinary market. It often means households pay without pooling, while still depending on professional advice they cannot fully check. That is a reason to study both evaluation methods and health financing in the same chapter.

> **EXAM TIP:** Define the health market problem in one line (patient is not a fully informed consumer paying at the point of use) → CAUSE table → one India line (OOPE plus agency). Do not write a full essay on Arrow unless asked.

PERSPECTIVE (VIEWPOINT)
State the viewpoint in the question. A chronic home-care programme may save the hospital and cost the family. A screening programme may look cheap to the facility and expensive to the Ministry once follow-up treatment is counted. Possible perspectives: patient; providing institution; programme; Ministry of Health; government (health plus other ministries); society.

A result that is attractive from a hospital perspective can be unattractive from a societal perspective, or the reverse. When the broader view prefers a programme that a facility will not adopt, a higher authority may need to change incentives.

KEY POINTS
- Health economics is the study of how scarce resources are allocated to produce, distribute, and consume health and health care.
- Economic evaluation is the comparative analysis of alternative courses of action in terms of both their costs and consequences.
- Opportunity cost is the benefit forgone in the next best use.
- Full evaluation always compares alternatives and always includes costs and consequences.
- Cost-of-illness studies and pure cost analyses are not full evaluations.
- HTAIn (DHR) is India's institutional home for HTA.
- Market failures in health are summarised by CAUSE.

FORMULAS AND CALCULATIONS
Net health benefit (concept): health gained minus health forgone elsewhere because extra resources were used.
