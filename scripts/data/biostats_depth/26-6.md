OVERVIEW OF STATISTICAL TESTS

Tests of statistical significance are mathematical devices used to obtain a p-value (or a test statistic to compare with a table) and to decide whether an observed difference or association is likely due to chance.

There are two families:
- Parametric tests: assume an approximate normal distribution (or a large n) and usually compare means on an interval or ratio scale.
- Non-parametric tests: fewer distributional assumptions; used for ranks, ordinal data, and categorical frequencies.

WHICH TEST WHEN

| Question | Data | Typical test |
|---|---|---|
| One large sample mean versus a known μ | Quantitative, n large or σ known | Z test for a mean |
| One small sample mean versus a known μ | Quantitative, σ unknown | One-sample t |
| Two independent means | Quantitative, ~normal | Unpaired (Student) t; Z if both samples large |
| Two paired means (before/after, matched) | Quantitative | Paired t |
| Three or more independent means | Quantitative | One-way ANOVA (F), then post-hoc |
| One proportion versus a known P | Categorical | Z for a proportion |
| Two independent proportions | Categorical | SE(p1 − p2) / Z, or χ² |
| Association in a contingency table | Categorical | Chi-square (Fisher if cells tiny) |
| Two independent ordinal / skewed samples | Ranks | Mann–Whitney (Wilcoxon rank-sum) |
| Paired ordinal / skewed | Ranks | Wilcoxon signed-rank (or sign test) |
| Three or more independent ranks | Ranks | Kruskal–Wallis |

Z is a large-sample (or known-σ) form of a mean or proportion test. t is used when σ is estimated from a small or moderate sample. There is no magic wall at n = 30, but that figure is the conventional teaching cut for "large."

PARAMETRIC TESTS FOR MEANS

1. Standard error of the mean and Z
Z = (x-bar − μ0) / (s / √n)

Used when n is large. Compare |Z| with 1.96 (5 percent, two-sided) or 2.58 (1 percent).

2. Standard error of the difference between two means (large samples)

SE(x1 − x2) = √(s1²/n1 + s2²/n2)

Z = (x1 − x2) / SE(x1 − x2)

Example pattern. Boys n = 169, mean height 168 cm, SD 14. Girls n = 54, mean 153, SD 8.
SE = √(196/169 + 64/54) = √(1.160 + 1.185) ≈ 1.53
Z = 15 / 1.53 ≈ 9.8, p < 0.001. Height differs with sex.

3. Student's t-test (small samples, σ unknown)

t = (x-bar − μ0) / (s / √n)    with df = n − 1

Unpaired (independent) two-sample t, equal-variance form:

t = (x1 − x2) / [s_p × √(1/n1 + 1/n2)]

s_p² = [(n1 − 1)s1² + (n2 − 1)s2²] / (n1 + n2 − 2)

df = n1 + n2 − 2

Paired t (same subjects before and after, or matched pairs):

Work on the differences d.

t = d-bar / (s_d / √n)    df = n − 1

Use paired t when each subject is his or her own control (breathing capacity before and after tapping ascites; haemoglobin before and after vitamin B12). Use unpaired t when two separate groups are compared (normal versus malnourished chest circumference).

4. Analysis of variance (ANOVA, F test)
Compares means of **three or more** independent groups by splitting total variance into between-group and within-group parts.

F = MS_between / MS_within

H0: all group means are equal. A significant F says at least one mean differs; it does not say which pair. Then use a post-hoc test (Tukey or Bonferroni-adjusted comparisons).

Why not many t-tests? Each extra pairwise t inflates Type I error.

Assumptions: independent observations, approximate normality of residuals, similar variances. If badly violated, consider Kruskal–Wallis.

NON-PARAMETRIC AND CATEGORICAL TESTS

Selection map (nominal versus ordinal):

| Groups | Nominal / frequencies | Ordinal / ranks |
|---|---|---|
| 1 (paired 2×2) | McNemar | Wilcoxon signed-rank |
| 2 independent | Chi-square (or Fisher) | Mann–Whitney (Wilcoxon rank-sum) |
| 3 or more independent | Chi-square (r × c) | Kruskal–Wallis |

Other named tests
- Cronbach's alpha: internal consistency of a questionnaire.
- Kolmogorov–Smirnov: assess whether a sample matches a stated distribution (including normality).
- Dixon Q: a simple outlier screen.
- Log-rank / Cox: compare survival curves (Kaplan–Meier).
- Bland–Altman: agreement of a new measurement with a reference, not correlation.
- Fisher exact: 2×2 table with small expected counts.

CHI-SQUARE TEST

A test of significance for association (or for goodness of fit) with **qualitative** (categorical) data. It tells whether an association is present, not how strong it is (strength needs a coefficient such as Cramer's V, or an odds ratio).

χ² = Σ [(O − E)² / E]

Expected cell: E = (row total × column total) / grand total

Degrees of freedom for a contingency table: df = (r − 1)(c − 1). For 2×2, df = 1. Critical value at df = 1, p = 0.05 is **3.84**.

Yates' continuity correction (2×2, small n): subtract 0.5 from |O − E| before squaring. Use when any expected cell is small (classic teaching: less than 5). If expected counts are very small, prefer Fisher exact.

Goodness of fit: compare observed class frequencies with frequencies expected from a ratio (for example 1 : 8 : 16) or from an earlier study. df = k − 1 (minus extra estimated parameters if any).

Worked 2×2 (vaccines)
Vaccine A: attacked 22, not attacked 68, n = 90.
Vaccine B: attacked 14, not attacked 72, n = 86.
Totals: attacked 36, not attacked 140, N = 176.

H0: no difference in attack rates.

E(A, attacked) = 90 × 36 / 176 = 18.41
E(A, not) = 90 × 140 / 176 = 71.59
E(B, attacked) = 86 × 36 / 176 = 17.59
E(B, not) = 86 × 140 / 176 = 68.41

χ² = (22−18.41)²/18.41 + (68−71.59)²/71.59 + (14−17.59)²/17.59 + (72−68.41)²/68.41 ≈ 1.79

df = 1, 1.79 < 3.84. Do not reject H0. The data do not show that vaccine B is superior.

STANDARD ERROR OF A PROPORTION AND OF THE DIFFERENCE

SE(p) = √(pq / n)

95 percent limits for a single proportion: p ± 1.96 SE(p).

Example. 10 percent eosinophils in 200 white cells: p = 0.10, SE = √(0.10×0.90/200) = 0.0212.
95 percent limits: 0.10 ± 1.96×0.0212 = 0.058 to 0.142, that is 5.8 percent to 14.2 percent.

SE of the difference of two independent proportions:

SE(p1 − p2) = √[p1 q1 / n1 + p2 q2 / n2]

(Some texts use a pooled p under H0 of equal proportions.)

Z = (p1 − p2) / SE(p1 − p2)

The same 2×2 data can usually be tested by Z or by χ²; χ² with df = 1 equals Z².

SPECIAL NOTES

- Cronbach's alpha, Kolmogorov–Smirnov, Bland–Altman, and Cox models are one-line viva tools; they are not substitutes for t or χ² in ordinary theory answers.
- Always end a numerical answer with a **sentence**: "The difference is / is not statistically significant at the 5 percent level; H0 is rejected / not rejected."

**Mnemonic:** CQ, TQ
- C: Chi-square is for Qualitative data (proportions, categories).
- T: t-test is for Quantitative data (means).

> **EXAM TIP:** Draw the which-test table first. For a long answer on t: define, unpaired versus paired, formula, df, one medical example each. For χ²: formula, E, df, 3.84, Yates, one 2×2. Never list Kruskal–Wallis under nominal data.

KEY POINTS

- Z / t / ANOVA compare means. χ² compares frequencies.
- Paired t uses differences. Unpaired t uses two samples. ANOVA is for three or more means.
- Mann–Whitney and Kruskal–Wallis are rank analogues of unpaired t and ANOVA.
- χ² = Σ(O − E)² / E; 2×2 critical value 3.84 at 5 percent.
- SE(p1 − p2) tests two percentages.

FORMULAS AND CALCULATIONS

- Z = (x-bar − μ) / (s / √n)
- SE(x1 − x2) = √(s1²/n1 + s2²/n2)
- t_paired = d-bar / (s_d / √n)
- t_unpaired = (x1 − x2) / [s_p √(1/n1 + 1/n2)]
- F = MS_between / MS_within
- χ² = Σ(O − E)² / E
- E = (row total × column total) / N
- df = (r − 1)(c − 1)
- SE(p) = √(pq / n)
- SE(p1 − p2) = √(p1q1/n1 + p2q2/n2)
