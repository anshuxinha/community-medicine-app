[EX]2. Write short notes on: (i) frequency distribution; (ii) relative frequency; (iii) cumulative frequency.[/EX]
[ANS]
**(i) Frequency distribution**
A table (or its graph) that shows how many observations fall in each class of a variable. After raw data are sorted, equal class intervals are chosen (commonly 6 to 16 groups) and tallies are converted to frequencies (f). It is the starting point for the mean of grouped data, the histogram, and the polygon.

**(ii) Relative frequency**
The class frequency divided by the total n, often as a percentage: (f / n) × 100. It lets two samples of different size be compared class by class.

**(iii) Cumulative frequency**
The running total of frequencies from the lowest class up (less-than type) or from the highest down (more-than type). Plotted against the class boundary it gives the ogive, from which median, quartiles, and any percentile are read.
[/ANS]

[EX]3. State which type of diagrams are used for presentation of: (i) quantitative data; (ii) qualitative data. Illustrate giving familiar examples.[/EX]
[ANS]
**(i) Quantitative (continuous) data**
Histogram (frequency of haemoglobin classes; no gaps). Frequency polygon or curve (same data, midpoints joined). Line diagram (year-wise incidence of measles). Ogive (cumulative heights of students). Scatter diagram (height versus weight). Box plot (quartiles of birth weight).

**(ii) Qualitative (discrete) data**
Simple or multiple bar (sex-wise admissions by year). Component or proportional bar (birth-order shares rural versus urban). Pie / sector diagram (types of leprosy among 240 patients). Pictogram (hospital beds). Spot or shaded map (cholera cases by street).

**Rule.** If you measured it on a scale, start with a histogram. If you counted a named category, start with a bar or a pie.
[/ANS]

[EX]5. Illustrate the following sterilization data (Family Planning Programme, India, figures in thousands) by a bar chart. The book asks for a component bar and the percentage written on top of the bar.[/EX]
[ANS]
**How to draw**
- X-axis: years 1969–70 through 1981–82 (13 bars).
- Each year is one **component bar** of height = target (thousands).
- Shade the lower part = achieved; the remainder = shortfall (or draw achieved as the filled component).
- Write the given percent achieved on top of each bar (64.2, 51.2, 105.2, 54.8, 41.6, 67.7, 107.1, 192.2, 23.8, 37.4, 58.3, 70.9, 96.4).

**What the chart shows**
Achievement crossed the target in 1971–72, 1975–76, and especially 1976–77 (192.2 percent). It collapsed in 1977–78 (23.8 percent) and recovered slowly toward 1981–82 (96.4 percent). A component bar plus the printed percentage is the correct qualitative-time presentation; a line diagram of percent achieved is an acceptable extra for trend.
[/ANS]

[EX]6. Present the live-births by order of birth (India, rural and urban, 1972 and 1978) by a component or proportional bar chart.[/EX]
[ANS]
Draw **four proportional bars** of equal height 100 percent (rural 1972, rural 1978, urban 1972, urban 1978). Divide each bar into birth orders 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6 or more using the printed percentages.

**Reading the figure**
- First-order shares rose (rural 19.85 to 23.45 percent; urban 21.60 to 25.65 percent).
- Sixth-or-more shares fell (rural 22.00 to 15.36 percent; urban 20.02 to 12.99 percent).
- Urban bars show a larger shift toward low order than rural bars.

This is the correct diagram: one whole (all live births) split into ordered qualitative parts, compared across place and year.

[EX]7. Illustrate progress in medical termination of pregnancy in India by a line chart using the tabulated year-wise numbers.[/EX]
[ANS]
**Data (numbers of MTP).** 1972–73: 20,143; 1973–74: 41,212; 1974–75: 1,05,531; 1975–76: 2,14,205; 1976–77: 2,78,870; 1977–78: 2,47,049; 1978–79: 3,17,732; 1979–80: 3,60,832; 1980–81: 3,88,405; 1981–82: 4,26,451.

[EX]8. Prepare a linear diagram of serum oxytetracycline after intramuscular and intravenous injection and draw conclusions.[/EX]
[ANS]
Plot time (1, 2, 12, 18, 24 hours) on X and serum level (μg/ml) on Y. Two lines: intramuscular (0.2, 1.5, 0.9, 0.4, 0.1) and intravenous (5.91, 3.39, 0.493, 0.035, 0.0076).

**Conclusions**
- Intravenous injection gives a high peak at 1 hour (5.91) and then falls steeply; by 18–24 hours the level is almost nil.
- Intramuscular injection peaks later (2 hours, 1.5) at a much lower height but remains measurable longer than the intravenous tail.
- For a rapid high blood level, intravenous is superior. For a more sustained low level, intramuscular is superior. The linear (line) diagram is the right graph because both series are the same variable followed through time.
[/ANS]

[EX]10. State briefly, giving reasons, the diagrams you can draw for: (i) year-wise and sex-wise distribution of children less than 5 years per family in a large town; (ii) number of persons using different contraceptives (diaphragm, jelly, Nirodh, pills, Saheli, copper T) in the same town, as a proportional bar.[/EX]
[ANS]
**(i)** Qualitative (year) × qualitative (sex) counts: a **multiple (compound) bar diagram**, two bars (boys, girls) grouped under each year. A line diagram with two lines is acceptable if the emphasis is trend. Not a histogram (the count is not a continuous measurement). Not a single pie (two factors).

**(ii)** Several methods as parts of one total: **one proportional (component) bar** of height 100 percent, sliced by method; or a pie. The question itself asks for a proportional bar. A cluster of simple bars would compare magnitudes but would not show shares of the same whole as clearly.
[/ANS]

[EX]11. Comment: (i) Bar and pie diagrams serve the same purpose. (ii) Histogram and bar diagrams are not the same.[/EX]
[ANS]
**(i)** Partly true. Both display qualitative frequencies. A pie shows shares of one total; a bar compares magnitudes and can handle many categories, grouped years, or components. They are interchangeable only for a few parts of one whole. They are not the same tool for trends or for two-way classifications.

**(ii)** True. A bar has gaps and is for discrete or named classes; height = frequency. A histogram has **no gaps** and is for continuous data; **area** = frequency. Swapping them is a standard examination error.
[/ANS]

[EX]26. (ii) Draw a histogram for the number of children born to 350 women (0: 171; 1: 82; 2: 50; 3: 25; 4: 13; 5: 7; 6: 2).[/EX]

The figure is sharply right-skewed: the 0-child column (171) dominates; the tail runs to 6 children (2 women). Label X as "number of children born" and Y as "number of women."
