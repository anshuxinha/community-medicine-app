OVERVIEW OF BIOSTATISTICS

A single measured or counted fact (for example, height of one person, birth weight of one baby) is a datum.
Statistics (plural) refers to aggregate numerical data or figures stated for multiple units.
The same word, Statistics (singular), refers to the scientific discipline: the collection, classification, summarization, analysis, interpretation, hypothesis testing, and drawing of inferences from data.

BIOSTATISTICS

Biostatistics is the term used when tools of statistics are applied to data derived from biological sciences such as medicine. It can also be called quantitative medicine.

Any science demands precision. High or low blood pressure has no meaning unless it is expressed in figures. Incidence of tuberculosis or a death rate in typhoid is stated in figures. Enlargement of spleen is measured in fingers' breadth. In nature, blood pressure, pulse rate, action of a drug, or any other measurement varies not only from person to person but also from group to group. The extent of this variability, whether it is by chance (biological or normal) or due to some external factor, is learnt by studying statistics as a science.

Variation more than natural limits may be pathological (abnormal) due to the play of certain external factors. Hence biostatistics may also be called a science of variation. Data after collection, lying in a haphazard mass, are of no use unless they are properly sorted, presented, compared, analyzed, and interpreted. They mean something more than figures: they give a dimension to the problem and even suggest the solution.

A medical student should not depend on a statistician for the entire analysis. For professional interpretation of results, the clinician should learn the application of methods, which do not require mathematics higher than school level. Guidance of a qualified statistician is still needed from the beginning of a scientific study until conclusions are drawn.

Medical statistics go under different names when applied in different fields. The terms overlap and are not exclusive of each other:

1. Health statistics: in public health or community health.
2. Medical statistics: in medicine related to the study of defect, injury, disease, efficacy of a drug, serum, and line of treatment.
3. Vital statistics: in demography, pertaining to vital events of births, marriages, and deaths.

APPLICATION AND USES OF BIOSTATISTICS AS A SCIENCE

In physiology and anatomy
- To define what is normal or healthy in a population and to find limits of normality in variables such as weight and pulse rate. The mean pulse rate is 72 per minute, but the limits of normality on either side of the mean have to be established.
- To find the difference between means and proportions of normal at two places or in different periods. The mean height of boys in one state may be less than in another. Whether this difference is due to chance (natural variation) or because of factors such as better nutrition has to be decided.
- To find the correlation between two variables X and Y such as height and weight: whether weight increases or decreases proportionately with height, and if so by how much.

In pharmacology
- To find the action of a drug given to animals or humans: whether the changes produced are due to the drug or by chance.
- To compare the action of two different drugs or two successive dosages of the same drug.
- To find the relative potency of a new drug with respect to a standard drug.

In medicine
- To compare the efficacy of a particular drug, operation, or line of treatment: the percentage cured, relieved, or died in the experiment and control groups is compared, and whether the difference is due to chance or otherwise is found by applying statistical techniques.
- To find an association between two attributes such as cancer and smoking, or filariasis and social class.
- To identify signs and symptoms of a disease or syndrome. Cough in typhoid may be found by chance; fever is found in almost every case. The proportional incidence of one symptom or another indicates whether it is a characteristic feature of the disease.

In community medicine and public health
- To test usefulness of sera and vaccines in the field: percentage of attacks or deaths among the vaccinated is compared with that among the unvaccinated to find whether the difference is statistically significant.
- In epidemiological studies, the role of a causative factor is statistically tested. Deficiency of iodine as an important cause of goitre in a community is confirmed only after comparing the incidence of goitre before and after giving iodized salt.
- Public health measures adopted are evaluated. Lowering of morbidity in typhoid after pasteurization of milk may be attributed to a clean milk supply only if it is statistically proved. A fall in birth rate may be the result of family planning methods, or due to a rise in living standards, increasing awareness, and a higher age of marriage.

By learning methods in biostatistics a student learns to evaluate articles published in medical journals or papers read in conferences, and understands the basic methods of observation in clinical practice or research.

APPLICATION AND USES OF BIOSTATISTICS AS FIGURES

Health and vital statistics are essential tools in demography, public health, medical practice, and community services. Recording of vital events in birth and death registers and of diseases in hospitals is book-keeping of the community. Such events, properly recorded, form the eyes and ears of a public health or medical administrator. Biostatistics as a science of figures will tell:

- What are the leading causes of death?
- What are the important causes of sickness?
- Whether a particular disease is rising or falling in severity and prevalence?
- Which age group, sex, social class, profession, or place is affected the most?
- The levels or standards of health reached.
- Age and sex composition of population in a community.
- Whether a particular population is rising, falling, aging, or ailing.
- Which health programme should be given priority and what will be the requirements for the same.

Then uses under four headings: anatomy/physiology, pharmacology, clinical medicine, community medicine. Close with "figures are the eyes and ears of the administrator."

COMMON STATISTICAL TERMS

Learn these terms, their symbols, and notations, and refer back as needed.

1. Variable: A characteristic that takes on different values in different persons, places, or things. A quantity that varies within limits, such as height, weight, blood pressure, or age. Denoted as X; an orderly series as X1, X2, X3, …, Xn. The suffix n is the number in the series. Sigma (Σ) stands for summation of observations.

2. Constant: Quantities that do not vary, such as π = 3.1416 and e = 2.7183. They do not require statistical study. In biostatistics, the mean, standard deviation, standard error, correlation coefficient, and proportion of a particular population are treated as constants of that population.

3. Observation: An event and its measurement, such as blood pressure (event) and 120 mmHg (measurement).

4. Observational unit: The source that gives observations, such as an object or person. In medical statistics the terms individuals or subjects are used more often.

5. Data: The plural of datum. A set of values recorded on one or more observational units. Data are the raw materials of statistics.

6. Population: The entire group of people or study elements (persons, things, or measurements) for which we have an interest at a particular time. Populations are determined by our sphere of interest. A population may be finite (fixed number of values) or infinite (an endless succession of values). It must be fully defined: all human beings, all families, all women of 15–45 years, all patients, all doctors, and so on. A statistical population may also be birth weights, haemoglobin levels, thermometer readings, or red blood cell counts.

7. Sampling unit: Each member of a population.

8. Sample: A part of a population. It is a group of sampling units that form part of a population, generally selected so as to be representative of the population whose variables are under study.

9. Parameter: A summary value or constant of a variable that describes the **population** (population mean, population standard deviation, population proportion). Denoted by Greek letters (μ, σ, π).

10. Statistic: A summary value calculated from the **sample** (sample mean, sample standard deviation, sample proportion). Denoted by Roman letters (x-bar, s, p). A statistic is used to estimate a parameter and may or may not be a valid estimate.

11. Parametric test: A test in which population constants such as means and variances are used, and data tend to follow one assumed or established distribution such as normal, binomial, or Poisson.

12. Nonparametric test: A test such as chi-square in which no constant of a population is used. Data do not follow any specific distribution and no distributional assumptions are made. To classify good, better, and best you may allocate arbitrary numbers or ranks to each category.

SOURCES OF DATA

The main sources for collection of medical statistics are experiments, surveys, and records. Experiments and surveys are applied to generate data needed for specific purposes. Records provide ready-made data for routine and continuous information.

Experiments
Performed in the laboratories of physiology, biochemistry, pharmacology, and clinical pathology, or in hospital wards for investigations and fundamental research. Data collected with a specific objective by one or more workers are compiled and analyzed. Results are used in dissertations, theses, and papers.

Surveys
Carried out for epidemiological studies in the field by trained teams to find the incidence or prevalence of health or disease situations in a community (incidence of malaria, prevalence of leprosy). Also used in operational research: assessment of existing conditions, and merits of different methods adopted to control a disease. They provide useful information on:
- Changing trends in health status, morbidity, mortality, nutritional status, environmental hazards, and health practices.
- Feedback that may modify policy and lead to redefinition of objectives.
- Timely warning of public health hazards.

Records
Maintained as a routine in registers or books over a long period for vital statistics (births, marriages, deaths) and for illnesses in hospitals. Data thus collected are used in demography and public health practice. They may be qualitative (stillbirths, persons with peptic ulcer) or quantitative (birth weights, haemoglobin readings). Regular administrative machinery is necessary to record the events.

DATA AND VARIABLES

A variable is any character, characteristic, or quality that varies.

Classification based on nature of data

Do not treat qualitative as a synonym of discrete, or quantitative as a synonym of continuous. Those are two different splits.

1. Qualitative (categorical) data
- There is no notion of magnitude or size of the characteristic because it cannot be measured on a metric scale.
- Persons with the same attribute are counted to form groups: attacked, escaped, died, cured, vaccinated, male, treated, on placebo.
- Only the frequency varies, not a measured size of the attribute. Survival remains the same; the number of survivors varies.
- Results are expressed as a ratio, proportion, percentage, or rate.
- Methods commonly employed: standard error of proportion and chi-square tests.
- Examples: sex, ABO blood group, treated versus not treated.

2. Quantitative (numerical) data
- The characteristic has a magnitude. It is counted or measured.
- There are two things that can vary: the value of the characteristic and the frequency of persons with that value.
- Methods commonly employed: mean, standard deviation, standard error of mean, t and Z tests, correlation and regression.
- Examples: serum cholesterol, weight, mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC), blood pressure, number of children.

Classification of quantitative data by how values occur
- Discrete quantitative: only isolated countable values (no meaningful in-between). Examples: parity, number of children, number of deaths in a year, platelet count.
- Continuous quantitative: any value in a range is possible (limited only by the measuring instrument). Examples: height, body temperature, haemoglobin, MUAC.

A count such as "number of children" is quantitative and discrete. It is not qualitative merely because it is a whole number.

Classification based on number of values
- Dichotomous (binary) variable: only 2 possible values. Examples: Rh group (positive or negative), sex (male or female), diabetes (present or absent).
- Polytomous variable: more than 2 possible values. Examples: ABO blood group; Body Mass Index (BMI) groups (underweight, normal, overweight, obese); anaemia grades. Raw MUAC or raw BMI in centimetres or kg/m² are continuous measurements, not polytomous labels, until they are cut into classes.

Classification based on dependency in a study
- Independent variable: the factor that is changed or used to predict. Example: the liquid used to water plants; dose of a drug.
- Dependent variable: the change that happens due to the independent variable. Example: height of the plants; fall in blood pressure.
- Controlled variable: a factor held unchanged. Example: the type of plant used.

SCALES OF MEASUREMENT (I.R.O.N. SCALE)

A useful exam mnemonic: Interval, Ratio, Ordinal, Nominal.

Categorical scales (qualitative variables)

1. Nominal scale: based on names. No sequence or specific order.
   - Examples: Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) status (positive or negative), race, blood groups, types of hospitals, sex.
   - Appropriate average: mode. Appropriate test family: chi-square.

2. Ordinal scale: a sequence or specific order is present. Used for grading into categories.
   - Examples: severity of anaemia (mild, moderate, severe); hypertension grades; social class; Child-Pugh score categories A, B, C.
   - Likert scale: a bipolar ordinal scale used to quantify attitudes (strongly agree to strongly disagree), usually 3, 5, or 7 points.
   - Appropriate average: median (or mode). Tests: rank-based (Wilcoxon, Mann–Whitney, Kruskal–Wallis).

Dimensional (metric) scales (quantitative variables)

Mean and standard deviation can be worked out only on an interval or ratio scale.

1. Interval scale: equal intervals, but absolute zero is absent. There is no true starting point.
   - Example: temperature in Celsius or Fahrenheit. 20 °C is not "twice as hot" as 10 °C.
   - Appropriate average for symmetric data: mean.

2. Ratio scale: equal intervals and an absolute zero is present. There is a true starting point.
   - Examples: temperature in Kelvin, weight, height, age, MUAC, blood pressure, pulse rate.
   - Ratios are meaningful: 80 kg is twice 40 kg.

**Mnemonic:** IRON
- I: Interval (no true zero)
- R: Ratio (true zero)
- O: Ordinal (ordered categories)
- N: Nominal (named categories)

> **EXAM TIP:** Define the scale, give two examples, then state which average and which test family fit. Draw a 4-row table (scale | example | zero | average). Do not confuse ordinal grades with interval measurements.

KEY POINTS

- Biostatistics is statistics applied to biological and medical data (quantitative medicine).
- Health, medical, and vital statistics overlap; they are not exclusive labels.
- A parameter describes a population (Greek). A statistic describes a sample (Roman).
- Data come from experiments, surveys, and records.
- A datum is one fact. Statistics (plural) are many figures. Statistics (singular) is the science of those figures.
- Qualitative data are named categories. Quantitative data are numbers (discrete counts or continuous measurements).
- Nominal and ordinal are categorical. Interval and ratio are metric.
- Mean and SD belong to interval or ratio scales. Median belongs to ordinal or skewed metric data. Mode belongs to nominal data.
