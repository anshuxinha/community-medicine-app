OVERVIEW OF DATA PRESENTATION

Data collected and compiled from experimental work, records, and surveys must be accurate and complete. Sorting or classification of data into characteristic groups or classes is the first step before presentation. Presentation methods must be concise without losing details, arouse interest, be simple and meaningful, define the problem, and suggest solutions.

There are two main methods of presenting frequencies of a variable: tabulation and drawing (charts and diagrams).

TABULATION

Tables are devices for presenting data from a mass of statistical data.

Rules for a good table
- Number tables sequentially.
- Give each table a clear title (what, where, when).
- Headings of columns and rows must be extremely clear (height in cm, age in years).
- Present data according to size, chronologically, or alphabetically.
- Footnotes may explain excluded data or abbreviations.
- Units belong in the heading, not repeated in every cell.

Types of tabulation
- Simple tables: one characteristic, such as year of admission and number of postgraduate students.
- Frequency distribution table: data split into class intervals and frequencies.
  - Number of groups: ideally a maximum of about 20 and a minimum of 5 (ordinarily 6 to 16, depending on the detail needed).
  - Class intervals should be equal throughout the table.
  - State whether the interval is inclusive or exclusive (for example 60 to less than 61).
- Master table: one line per person; all subjective and objective information opposite identification data.

FREQUENCY, RELATIVE FREQUENCY, AND CUMULATIVE FREQUENCY

- Frequency (f): number of observations falling in a class.
- Relative frequency: f / n, often written as a percentage. Useful when two samples of different size are compared.
- Cumulative frequency: running total of frequencies from the lowest class upward (less-than type) or from the highest downward (more-than type). Needed to draw an ogive and to locate median, quartiles, and percentiles.

CHARTS AND DIAGRAMS FOR QUALITATIVE DATA

Bar chart / bar diagram
- Used to compare the magnitude of different frequencies in discrete or qualitative data.
- The most versatile of all statistical diagrams.
- Rectangular bars represent data. Bars may be vertical or horizontal.
- Bars are usually separated by spaces nearly equal to half the width of the bar.
- For visual comparison of magnitudes. Not used to show the relationship between two continuous variables (that is a scatter or a line of regression).
- Types:
  - Simple bar chart: one bar per category.
  - Multiple / compound bar chart: two or more bars grouped (males and females per year).
  - Component / proportional bar chart: each bar divided into parts; each part is a proportion. Write the percentage on top of the bar when asked.

Pie chart / sector diagram
- Discrete data of qualitative characters (blood groups, causes of mortality, types of leprosy).
- Frequencies shown in a circle. The angle of the sector is proportional to the frequency.
- Formula: angle (degrees) = (class frequency / total observations) × 360.
- Gives a comparative difference at a glance. Avoid when there are many tiny slices.

Pictogram / picture diagram
- Popular method to impress the frequency of events on the common person.
- Pictures or symbols represent counts (one human figure = 1,000 persons).

Statistical maps (spot map, shaded map, dot-density map)
- Geographical distribution of frequencies.
- Classic example: John Snow's cholera spot map.

CHARTS AND DIAGRAMS FOR QUANTITATIVE DATA

Histogram
- Used for continuous quantitative data.
- Pictorial diagram of a frequency distribution.
- Series of adjacent rectangles with **no spaces** between them.
- Class intervals on the horizontal axis (abscissa); frequencies on the vertical axis (ordinate).
- It is an **area** diagram: the area of each rectangle is proportional to the frequency. If class widths are unequal, height must be adjusted so that area still equals frequency.

Frequency polygon
- Area diagram of frequency distribution developed over a histogram.
- Join the midpoints of class intervals at the heights of the frequencies with straight lines.
- Close the polygon to the baseline at the midpoints of the empty classes on either side.

Frequency curve
- When the number of observations is very large and the group interval is reduced, the polygon loses its angulation and becomes a smooth frequency curve.

Line chart / line diagram
- A special frequency polygon drawn between a value (or frequency) and time.
- Shows the trend of an event over a period (rising, falling, or fluctuating).
- Best method to show the trend of incidence or a programme indicator with the passage of time.
- Does **not** replace a scatter diagram for the relationship between two measured variables.

Cumulative frequency diagram (ogive)
- Plot cumulative frequency against the upper (or lower) class boundary.
- The curve is always uprising for a less-than ogive.
- Helps in defining cut-off limits and locating percentiles, quartiles, and the median.
- The less-than ogive and the greater-than ogive intersect at the median.

Scatter diagram / dot diagram
- Relationship between two continuous variables.
- Vertical axis generally represents the dependent or outcome variable.
- A cluster around a straight line suggests a linear relationship. No cluster means no relationship.
- Indicates strong positive, weak positive, or negative correlation visually. It does not give the numerical coefficient r.

OTHER IMPORTANT PLOTS

Stem and leaf plot
- Frequency distribution in which stems are the tens place and leaves are the ones place. A stem of 1 and a leaf of 2 represents 12.

Tree diagram
- Possible outcomes of an event (used in probability and clinical decision paths).

Box and whisker plot (related to tiles)
- Tertile: 2 intercepts, 3 tiles.
- Quartile: 3 intercepts, 4 tiles. Represented by a box plot.
- The box contains the middle 50 percent of values (interquartile range). Each whisker covers 25 percent unless outliers are plotted separately.
- Components: minimum, lower quartile (Q1), median (Q2), upper quartile (Q3), maximum, interquartile range (IQR = Q3 − Q1).
- Pentile (4 intercepts), decile (9), centile / percentile (99 intercepts, 100 equal parts). Median is the 50th centile.
- Percentile location in an ordered series of n values: position of Px = (n + 1) × x / 100.

HOW TO CHOOSE A DIAGRAM

| Data / question | Diagram |
|---|---|
| Categories, counts | Simple or multiple bar |
| Parts of a whole (few classes) | Pie / sector, or component bar |
| Continuous frequency distribution | Histogram, then polygon or curve |
| Trend over years | Line diagram |
| Two measured variables | Scatter |
| Geography | Spot or shaded map |
| Lay audience | Pictogram |
| Median and spread, outliers | Box and whisker |

Bar versus histogram (classic viva)
- Bar: qualitative or discrete; gaps between bars; height = frequency.
- Histogram: continuous; no gaps; **area** = frequency.

Bar versus pie
- Both can show the same qualitative frequencies. A bar is better for comparing magnitudes. A pie is better for showing shares of a total. They serve a similar purpose only when the question is "parts of one whole."

> **EXAM TIP:** First say table, then diagram. Always match diagram to data type. Write the pie-angle formula if a sector diagram is asked. For histogram versus bar: continuous and no gaps versus categorical with gaps.

KEY POINTS

- Classify first, then tabulate, then draw.
- Equal class intervals; 6 to 16 groups is a practical range.
- Qualitative: bar, pie, pictogram, map.
- Quantitative: histogram, polygon, curve, line (time), ogive, scatter.
- Ogive locates median, quartiles, and percentiles.
- Histogram is an area diagram.

FORMULAS AND CALCULATIONS

- Pie angle (degrees) = (class frequency / n) × 360
- Relative frequency = f / n
- Cumulative frequency of class k = f1 + f2 + … + fk
- Position of percentile Px in ordered data = (n + 1) × x / 100
