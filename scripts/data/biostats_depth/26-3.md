OVERVIEW

After collection and presentation, quantitative data are summarized by answering three questions:
1. What is the average or central value?
2. How are the other values dispersed around this value?
3. What is the shape of the distribution: is it normal?

Normal is not the mean alone. It is the accepted range of variation on either side of the average. Normal blood pressure is not "the mean 120" but a range such as mean 120 ± 20, that is 100 to 140, with still some chance that higher or lower values are compatible with health.

[LAQ]Measures of central tendency and measures of dispersion[/LAQ]

MEASURES OF CENTRAL TENDENCY (AVERAGES)

An average is the one central value around which all other observations are dispersed. The **median** splits an ordered series so that half the observations lie above it and half below it. The mean does that only when the distribution is symmetric. An average helps:
- To see that most normal observations lie close to the centre, while a few too large or too small lie at the ends.
- To compare groups: mean incubation of cholera versus typhoid; mean daily attendance of two hospitals. After finding the difference, one reasons why.

There are three common measures of central position: mean, median, and mode.

MEAN (ARITHMETIC AVERAGE)

The sum of all observations divided by the number of observations. Denoted x-bar for a sample and μ for a population.

x-bar = ΣX / n

It uses every observation and is the average most used in further tests of significance. It is the measure most affected by extreme values.

Types of mean
- Arithmetic mean (AM): the ordinary average.
- Geometric mean (GM): useful for exponentially growing data and for data with extreme values (titres, some rates). GM = (X1 × X2 × … × Xn)^(1/n).
- Harmonic mean (HM): useful for rates and for data that are inverses (average speed). HM = n / Σ(1/Xi).

Ungrouped series
- Small numbers: add and divide by n.
- Large numbers: assume a working origin w (assumed mean). Let x = X − w. Then x-bar = w + (Σx / n).

Example (direct). Erythrocyte sedimentation rates of 7 subjects: 7, 5, 3, 4, 6, 4, 5.
ΣX = 34, n = 7, mean = 34 / 7 = 4.86.

Example (assumed mean). Incubation periods of 9 polio cases around w = 20: deviations sum to 0, so mean = 20 + 0 = 20 days.

Grouped series: weighted mean
When n is large, prepare a frequency table. The mean is a weighted mean (mean of means):

x-bar = Σ(f × Xg) / n

where Xg is the midpoint of the class and f is the class frequency. Do **not** average class means without weights.

Example. Average income of 10 lady doctors is Rs 40,000 and of 20 male doctors is Rs 60,000. The combined mean is not (40,000 + 60,000) / 2 = 50,000. It is (40,000 × 10 + 60,000 × 20) / 30 = 16,00,000 / 30 = Rs 53,333.

Working-unit shortcut for grouped data
1. Place 0 opposite a convenient central class (working origin w = that class midpoint).
2. Code other classes as −2, −1, 0, +1, +2, … in units of the class interval i.
3. Mean in working units = Σ(f x) / n.
4. Mean in real units: x-bar = w + [Σ(f x) / n] × i.

Never forget to multiply the coded mean by the class interval when i is not 1.

MEDIAN

The middle observation after the series is arranged in ascending or descending order.

- Odd n: the middle value. Serial number of the median = (n + 1) / 2.
- Even n: the mean of the two central values.

Median is preferred when one or more of the lowest or highest observations are wide apart (hospital stay, income, age at marriage in a skewed series). Mean of 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, 32 is 12.3; median is 10. The value 32 distorts the mean.

Grouped series: locate the class that contains n/2, then interpolate:

Median = L + [(n/2 − cf) / f] × i

where L is the lower boundary of the median class, cf is the cumulative frequency before that class, f is the frequency of the median class, and i is the class width.

MODE

The most frequently occurring observation (the most fashionable value). Example: in tuberculin reactions 3, 5, 7, 7, 8, 8, 8, 10, 11, 12 the mode is 8 mm.

Mode is rarely used alone in medical statistics. A series may be bimodal (two peaks), which often means two dissimilar groups are mixed.

Empirical relation (moderately skewed unimodal data):

Mode ≈ 3 Median − 2 Mean

WHICH AVERAGE TO USE

| Scale / shape | Prefer |
|---|---|
| Nominal | Mode |
| Ordinal | Median |
| Metric, roughly symmetric | Mean (with SD) |
| Metric, skewed or open-ended | Median (with IQR) |
| Combining group averages | Weighted mean |

> **EXAM TIP:** Define average, then mean / median / mode with one worked mini-example each. Stress weighted mean for grouped data. Close with the scale table.

[SN]Measures of variability[/SN]

MEASURES OF DISPERSION (VARIABILITY)

An average without a measure of scatter is incomplete. Types of variability taught in medical statistics:
- Biological (normal) variability: pulse, blood pressure, height among healthy people.
- Real (pathological) variability: beyond biological limits, due to disease or an external factor.
- Experimental / observer / instrument variability: parallax, uncalibrated sphygmomanometer.
- Sampling variability: sample statistics differ from sample to sample (measured later as standard error).

1. RANGE
Highest minus lowest. Simple, but ignores all intermediate values and is unstable in small samples.

2. MEAN DEVIATION (AVERAGE DEVIATION)
Average of absolute deviations from the mean.

MD = Σ |X − mean| / n

3. VARIANCE
Mean of squared deviations.

Population: σ² = Σ(X − μ)² / N
Sample (unbiased): s² = Σ(X − x-bar)² / (n − 1)

The divisor n − 1 is Bessel's correction (degrees of freedom). Classic teaching sometimes uses n when n is large (greater than 30).

4. STANDARD DEVIATION (SD)
The root mean square deviation from the arithmetic mean. The principal measure of spread around the mean. Denoted σ (population) or s (sample).

s = √[ Σ(X − x-bar)² / (n − 1) ]

It uses every observation, unlike the range. It is the unit of distance on the baseline of the normal curve.

Assumed-mean computing formula (ungrouped):

s = √[ (Σx² − (Σx)² / n) / (n − 1) ]

where x = X − w.

Grouped:

s = i × √[ (Σf x² − (Σf x)² / n) / (n − 1) ]

5. COEFFICIENT OF VARIATION (CV)
Relative variation, so two series in different units or of different magnitude can be compared.

CV = (SD / mean) × 100 (percent)

Example. Systolic blood pressure: children mean 100, SD 8, CV = 8 percent. Adults mean 120, SD 12, CV = 10 percent. Adults are relatively more variable.

6. STANDARD ERROR (SE)
Not a measure of scatter **within** one sample. It measures how much a sample statistic (usually the mean) is expected to vary from sample to sample. Bridge to confidence intervals and tests.

SE of mean = SD / √n

SE of a proportion = √(pq / n)

Percentiles, quartiles, and the semi-interquartile range belong with location as well as spread. Q1, Q2 (median), Q3 divide the ordered series into four equal parts. Semi-interquartile range (SIQR) = (Q3 − Q1) / 2. IQR = Q3 − Q1 is less sensitive to extremes than the range.

> **EXAM TIP:** Define variability, then list range, MD, variance, SD, CV, and SE with one line each on **when** to use them. CV is the answer whenever units differ. SE is sampling variation, not biological spread.

KEY POINTS

- Mean uses all data and feeds significance tests. Median resists outliers. Mode is the commonest value.
- Grouped mean is always weighted. Multiply coded means by the class interval.
- SD is the working measure of dispersion. CV compares relative spread. SE judges the sample mean.
- Empirical: Mode ≈ 3 Median − 2 Mean.

FORMULAS AND CALCULATIONS

- x-bar = ΣX / n
- Grouped: x-bar = Σ f Xg / n = w + (Σ f x / n) × i
- Median (raw): middle of the ordered series
- Median (grouped): L + [(n/2 − cf) / f] × i
- Mode ≈ 3 Median − 2 Mean
- Range = Xmax − Xmin
- MD = Σ |X − mean| / n
- s² = Σ(X − x-bar)² / (n − 1)
- s = √s²
- CV = (s / x-bar) × 100
- SE(mean) = s / √n
- SIQR = (Q3 − Q1) / 2
