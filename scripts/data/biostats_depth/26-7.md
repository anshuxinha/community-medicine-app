OVERVIEW

Probability is the language of chance. Correlation and regression describe how two quantitative variables move together, and how one may be predicted from the other.

[SN]Probability[/SN]

[LAQ]Laws of probability and binomial distribution[/LAQ]

PROBABILITY (CHANCE)

Probability is the relative frequency with which an event is expected to occur on average. It ranges from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain). It can never be negative and cannot exceed 1.

In equally likely cases: P(A) = number of favourable outcomes / number of possible outcomes.

LAWS OF PROBABILITY

1. Multiplication law (AND / BOTH)
Used when two or more events occur together and are **independent** (not associated).

P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B)

Mnemonic often taught: "AND / BOTH: multiply."

2. Addition law (EITHER / OR / ANY)
Used when events are **mutually exclusive** (one excludes the other).

P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B)

If events can occur together (not exclusive):

P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B)

Example. Gastric cancer prevalence 10 percent (0.10). Helicobacter pylori 40 percent (0.40) in the same population, treated as independent for the exercise.
- Cancer AND H. pylori: 0.10 × 0.40 = 0.04
- Cancer OR H. pylori (if exclusive in the exercise sense): 0.10 + 0.40 = 0.50
If they can coexist, the exclusive-or form overstates the union; subtract the joint probability.

CONDITIONAL PROBABILITY AND SCREENING

P(A | B) = P(A and B) / P(B)

Screening 2×2 (expand on first use in this section):

|  | Disease present | Disease absent |
|---|---|---|
| Test positive | True positive (TP) | False positive (FP) |
| Test negative | False negative (FN) | True negative (TN) |

- Sensitivity: P(positive | disease) = TP / (TP + FN)
- Specificity: P(negative | no disease) = TN / (TN + FP)
- Predictive value positive: P(disease | positive) = TP / (TP + FP)
- Predictive value negative: P(no disease | negative) = TN / (TN + FN)
- Accuracy: (TP + TN) / total

BINOMIAL PROBABILITY

When each independent trial has two outcomes (success with probability p, failure q = 1 − p), the number of successes X in n trials follows a binomial distribution:

P(X = r) = C(n, r) × p^r × q^(n−r)

where C(n, r) = n! / [r! (n−r)!]

Example. Prevalence of diabetes 1 in 10, so p = 0.1, q = 0.9. Four children (n = 4).
- All 4 normal: C(4,0) × 0.1^0 × 0.9^4 = 0.6561
- 1 diabetic, 3 normal: C(4,1) × 0.1 × 0.9^3 = 0.2916
- 2 and 2: C(4,2) × 0.1² × 0.9² = 0.0486
- 3 diabetic, 1 normal: C(4,3) × 0.1³ × 0.9 = 0.0036
- All 4 diabetic: 0.1^4 = 0.0001

ODDS AND POISSON

Odds = P / (1 − P). If P = 0.20, odds = 0.25 (1 to 4).

Poisson distribution: probability of a given number of events in a fixed time (or space) when events are rare and independent. Mean equals variance (λ).

[SN]Correlation and regression[/SN]

[LAQ]Correlation coefficient and regression[/LAQ]

CORRELATION

Correlation is the relationship between two quantitative (continuous) variables. It is pictured by a scatter diagram. The correlation coefficient measures **strength and direction**, not causation.

Pearson's product-moment coefficient (r)
- Used for ungrouped, approximately normal, linearly related pairs.
- Range: −1 to +1.
- r = +1: perfect positive linear relation. r = −1: perfect negative. r = 0: no linear relation.
- |r| > 0.5 is often taught as a strong linear relationship; always test significance against the table for the given df = n − 2.

r = Σ(x y) / √[Σx² Σy²]

where x = X − x-bar, y = Y − y-bar.

Spearman's rank coefficient (rho)
- Used when data are ordinal, markedly non-normal, or you prefer ranks.
- It is **not** a coefficient of curvature. A curved (U-shaped) relation can have Pearson r near 0 even when a relationship exists; look at the scatter.

Direction
- Positive: X up, Y up (height and weight; systolic pressure and serum cholesterol).
- Negative: X up, Y down (age and forced expiratory volume; infant mortality and socioeconomic status).
- None: blink rate and blood pressure.

Coefficient of determination = r². It is the proportion of variability in one variable statistically associated with the other.

Multiple correlation: one dependent variable with two or more independent variables.

REGRESSION

Regression means **prediction**. The value of one variable is estimated from another related variable.

Straight line:

Y = a + b X

- Y: dependent (predicted) variable
- X: independent variable
- b (byx): slope, the regression coefficient of Y on X (change in Y per unit X)
- a: intercept, a = Y-bar − b × X-bar

b_yx = Σ(x y) / Σx²

Once b and a are known, expected Yc can be computed for each X and compared with observed Y.

Types
- Linear regression: quantitative Y. Univariate (one X) or multivariate (several X).
- Logistic regression: qualitative (binary) Y, such as myocardial infarction present or absent.

Significance of r
Compare r with the tabulated critical value for df = n − 2, or use

t = r √(n − 2) / √(1 − r²)    df = n − 2

A large |r| in a tiny sample may still be non-significant.

Utility of a scatter
- See direction and linearity before computing r.
- Spot outliers that inflate or deflate r.
- After fitting the line, points inside ±2 residual SD are compatible with the fitted relation at about 95 percent.

> **EXAM TIP:** Probability: define, then AND = multiply (independent), OR = add (exclusive). One binomial expansion. Correlation: scatter first, then r between −1 and +1, then "not causation." Regression: write Y = a + bx and say b is the slope. Spearman is ranks, not "curved r."

KEY POINTS

- 0 ≤ P ≤ 1. Independent joint events: multiply. Exclusive alternatives: add.
- Binomial: n independent yes/no trials.
- Sensitivity and specificity are conditional probabilities from a 2×2.
- r measures linear association. r² is shared variance. Regression predicts Y from X.
- Correlation does not prove causation.

FORMULAS AND CALCULATIONS

- P(A and B) = P(A) P(B)   (independent)
- P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B)   (exclusive)
- P(X = r) = C(n, r) p^r q^(n−r)
- Odds = P / (1 − P)
- Se = TP / (TP + FN); Sp = TN / (TN + FP)
- r = Σxy / √(Σx² Σy²)
- b_yx = Σxy / Σx²
- a = Y-bar − b X-bar
- t_r = r √(n − 2) / √(1 − r²)
