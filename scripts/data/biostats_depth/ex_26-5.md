[EX]30. Explain: (i) standard error of mean; (ii) confidence limits; (iii) null hypothesis; (iv) level of significance.[/EX]
[ANS]
**(i) Standard error of the mean.** SEM = SD / √n. It is the SD of the sampling distribution of the mean: how much x-bar is expected to bounce from sample to sample. It is not the SD of the raw observations.

**(ii) Confidence limits.** The two ends of a confidence interval. Large-sample 95 percent limits for a mean: x-bar ± 1.96 SEM (often taught as ± 2 SE). About 95 percent of such intervals from repeated samples would contain μ.

**(iii) Null hypothesis (H0).** The working assumption of no real difference (or no association) beyond chance. Tests try to reject it.

**(iv) Level of significance (α).** The pre-set risk of a Type I error, usually 0.05. If p < α, H0 is rejected.
[/ANS]

[EX]32. A random sample of 900 children had mean fat-fold thickness at triceps 3.4 mm, SD 2.3 mm. Can it reasonably be regarded as a representative sample of a population whose mean thickness is 3.2 mm? Discuss estimation of a population mean from a sample.[/EX]
[ANS]
SEM = 2.3 / √900 = 2.3 / 30 = 0.0767
Z = (3.4 − 3.2) / 0.0767 = 2.61
|Z| = 2.61 > 1.96, p < 0.01.

**Conclusion.** The sample mean is significantly higher than 3.2 mm. It should **not** be regarded as a representative sample of that universe (printed key: No; Z = 2.61, p < 0.01).

**Estimation.** The sample mean estimates μ. A 95 percent CI is 3.4 ± 1.96 × 0.0767 = 3.25 to 3.55 mm, which excludes 3.2 and agrees with the test.
[/ANS]

[EX]33. Ten random weights (kg): 68, 63, 66, 69, 63, 67, 70, 70, 71, 71. Does this sample adequately represent a population of mean 66 kg?[/EX]
[ANS]
n = 10, ΣX = 678, mean = 67.8
s = 3.01 (sample)
SEM = 3.01 / √10 = 0.95
t = (67.8 − 66) / 0.95 = 1.89
df = 9; two-sided 5 percent critical t ≈ 2.262.
|t| = 1.89 < 2.262, p > 0.05.

**Yes.** 66 kg lies inside the sample's 95 percent interval (printed key: Yes; t = 1.89, SE = 0.95). The sample is compatible with a universe mean of 66 kg.
[/ANS]

[EX]34. Define standard error of the mean. Mean haemoglobin of the population is 14.3. Can a sample of 15 individuals with mean 13.5 and SD 1.5 come from that population?[/EX]
[ANS]
SEM = SD / √n, the sampling SD of the mean.

SEM = 1.5 / √15 = 0.387
t = (13.5 − 14.3) / 0.387 = −2.07
df = 14; 5 percent critical t ≈ 2.145.
|t| = 2.07 < 2.145, p > 0.05.

**Yes**, the sample can come from that population (printed key: Yes; t = 2.05, SE = 0.39, p > 0.05). The shortfall of 0.8 g/dL is still compatible with chance in a sample of 15.
[/ANS]

[EX]35. Universe mean attacks of common cold 4.5, SD 2.87. Sample of 20: 7,2,5,0,1, 5,5,6,7,6, 2,6,9,4,8, 8,6,7,9,3. (i) Mean, SD, and SE of the mean. (ii) Why SD and SE differ. (iii) 95 percent confidence limits of attacks in the universe; do all sample observations lie inside them?[/EX]
[ANS]
**(i)** n = 20, ΣX = 106, mean = **5.30**
s = **2.59**, SEM = 2.59 / √20 = **0.58**

**(ii)** SD is the scatter of the **20 individual counts** around 5.30. SE is the scatter expected of the **sample mean** if the study were repeated. SE is always smaller (SD / √n).

**(iii)** 95 percent CI for the universe mean: 5.30 ± 1.96 × 0.58 = **4.16 to 6.44** (printed key 4.08 and 6.52, using a slightly different s or t0.05,19 = 2.093: 5.30 ± 2.093 × 0.58 = 4.09 to 6.51).

Individual observations run from 0 to 9. They need **not** all lie inside the CI of the **mean**. The CI is for μ, not for every person. Several counts (0, 1, 8, 9) lie outside 4–6.5 and that is expected.
[/ANS]
