OVERVIEW

Range, averages, and percentiles all describe a series, but they do not by themselves tell the **probability** that a new observation is usual. For that one needs the shape of the distribution around the mean, expressed in units of standard deviation (SD). The reference shape is the normal (Gaussian) distribution.

So much physiological variation exists that an isolated observation should not be labelled abnormal without referring it to mean and SD.

[SN]Normal distribution and normal curve[/SN]

[LAQ]Normal curve, standard normal deviate, and skewness[/LAQ]

NORMAL DISTRIBUTION (GAUSSIAN DISTRIBUTION)

When a large number of observations of a variable (height, blood pressure, pulse, birth weight) are taken at random and class intervals are kept small, it is seen that:
- Some observations lie above the mean and some below.
- Frequencies are greatest around the mean and decrease smoothly toward both extremes.
- Almost half the observations lie above and half below the mean, and the two sides are symmetrical.

A distribution of this shape is a normal or Gaussian distribution. It is a virtue of a large random sample.

Areas under a normal curve (memorize)

If the variable has mean μ and standard deviation σ:

| Interval | Area included | Outside |
|---|---|---|
| μ ± 1 σ | 68.27 percent (about two-thirds) | about 32 percent |
| μ ± 1.96 σ | 95 percent | 5 percent |
| μ ± 2 σ | 95.45 percent | 4.55 percent |
| μ ± 2.58 σ | 99 percent | 1 percent |
| μ ± 3 σ | 99.73 percent | 0.27 percent |

Thus six standard deviations (three on either side of the mean) cover almost the entire range. Values farther than 2 SD from the mean are uncommon (about 5 percent). Values farther than 3 SD are rare (about 0.3 percent) and may be unusual or pathological. "Normal" here means **usual**, not a clinical diagnosis.

Worked illustration. Heights of students, mean 160 cm, SD 5 cm.
- Mean ± 1 SD = 155 to 165 cm contains about 68 percent.
- Mean ± 2 SD = 150 to 170 cm contains about 95 percent.
- Mean ± 3 SD = 145 to 175 cm contains about 99.7 percent.
A height of 177 cm lies beyond mean + 3 SD; the chance that it belongs to this population is very small.

THE NORMAL CURVE

A histogram of a large normal sample with a small class interval becomes a smooth, symmetrical, bell-shaped (or "breast-shaped") curve.

Characteristics
1. Bell-shaped.
2. Bilaterally symmetrical.
3. Mean, median, and mode coincide at the centre.
4. The curve is asymptotic: the tails approach the baseline but never touch it.
5. Two points of inflection: the curve changes from convex to concave. A perpendicular from each inflection meets the baseline at a distance of **one SD** from the mean.
6. Total area under the curve = 1 (or 100 percent).

These properties belong to **every** normal curve of a variable X ~ N(μ, σ²). They do **not** mean that the mean is 0 and the SD is 1. That is true only after standardizing (see Z).

[SN]Z-score or standard normal deviate[/SN]

RELATIVE OR STANDARD NORMAL DEVIATE (Z)

Deviation from the mean, measured in units of SD, is the standard normal deviate Z.

Z = (X − μ) / σ

Z states how many SDs an observation is above or below the mean.
- Z = +1: X is one SD above the mean. About 84 percent of values lie below it; about 16 percent lie above it.
- Z = −1.5: only about 7 percent of values lie below X.
- Z = 0: X equals the mean.

The **standard normal** variable is Z ~ N(0, 1): mean 0, SD 1, variance 1. Areas for Z are read from the unit normal table (single tail).

Example 1. Mean birth weight 3.05 kg, SD 0.39 kg.
Normal limits at ±1.96 SD: 3.05 ± 1.96 × 0.39 = 2.29 to 3.81 kg.
- 4 kg is greater than 3.81, so it is unusual (outside 95 percent limits).
- 2.5 kg lies inside 2.29–3.81, so it is not labelled unusual on this rule.

Example 2. Menstrual cycle mean 28 days, SD 2 days.
Z for 30 days = (30 − 28) / 2 = +1. Table area beyond Z = 1 is 0.1587. About 16 percent of women have a cycle longer than 30 days.
Z for 22 days = (22 − 28) / 2 = −3. Area beyond is 0.0013 (0.13 percent).

Example 3. Pulse mean 72 / min, SD 3.5.
The central 50 percent of a normal curve lies between mean ± 0.6745 SD (often taught as 0.67 SD): 72 ± 2.3, that is about 69.7 to 74.3.

SKEWNESS (ASYMMETRY)

Not all biological series are symmetric. Duration of labour in a first child is typically skewed to the right (long right tail). Age at death is often bimodal (peaks in infancy and in old age), which suggests two dissimilar groups mixed in one population.

- No skew (symmetric): mean = median = mode.
- Positive (right) skew: long tail to the right. Mean is pulled toward the high values. Mean > median > mode.
- Negative (left) skew: long tail to the left. Mean < median < mode.

Pearson's measures
- First (mode) coefficient: (mean − mode) / SD
- Second (median) coefficient: 3 (mean − median) / SD

For skewed data, prefer the median and a non-parametric test rather than mean ± SD.

KURTOSIS

Kurtosis describes tailedness and peakedness relative to a normal curve.
- Mesokurtic: normal-like.
- Leptokurtic: heavier tails or sharper peak.
- Platykurtic: lighter tails or flatter peak.

Skewness is left-right asymmetry. Kurtosis is not skewness.

> **EXAM TIP:** Draw a labelled bell with 68 / 95 / 99.7. Write Z = (X − mean) / SD. State that mean 0 and SD 1 apply to the **standard** normal, not to every normal curve. Add one sentence on right versus left skew.

KEY POINTS

- Large random samples of many biological measurements follow a normal distribution.
- μ ± 1.96 σ covers 95 percent. μ ± 3 σ covers almost all.
- Z converts any normal X to the unit normal.
- Right skew: mean greater than median. Left skew: mean less than median.
- Bimodality suggests a mixed (heterogeneous) sample.

FORMULAS AND CALCULATIONS

- Z = (X − μ) / σ
- 95 percent usual limits ≈ μ ± 1.96 σ
- 99 percent usual limits ≈ μ ± 2.58 σ
- Pearson skewness (median form) = 3 (mean − median) / SD
