OVERVIEW

Designing an experiment in a hospital or laboratory, or an enquiry in the field, needs logical and systematic planning. Postgraduates and field investigators must be able to answer questions on purpose, scope, objectives, hypothesis, methodology, conclusions, and practical applicability. Size and nature of the sample, type of matching control, and selection of that control deserve early thought.

A design is a series of guide-posts. It may be tentative. New links in the data may force a modification. Working out the plan means deciding what, where, when, how much, and by what means.

Involve a biostatistician from the **beginning**, not only at the analysis stage. Late consultation cannot repair poor planning or inadequate data. Experts from related fields (sanitarian, entomologist, dentist, veterinarian) should be consulted when the question crosses disciplines.

STEPS IN METHODOLOGY AND DESIGNING

1. Definition of the problem
State the problem precisely: typhoid mortality after chloramphenicol; iron by injection versus by mouth; standard regimen in tuberculosis; pregnancy prevalence; rising malaria; smoking and lung cancer; cholesterol and coronary disease; 75 mg aspirin in prevention of stroke; optimum midwife population.

2. Aims and objectives
Say whether the nature of the problem is to be described or a solution compared. Examples: compare two lines of treatment; modify malaria control if incidence is rising; warn against smoking if it is shown to be a causative factor; compare laparoscopy with existing approaches for tubectomy.

3. Review of literature
Find whether the work has been done. Clarify whether you wish to confirm findings, challenge a conclusion, extend the work, or fill a gap. Example: microfilaria density is higher at night; which part of the night?

4. Hypothesis
After the problem, purpose, and literature are clear, state a precise assumption, positive or negative: iron by injection is not more effective than by mouth; there is no relationship between hypertension and social class; birth rate and infant mortality are lower in Integrated Child Development Services (ICDS) projects than outside them; cholera vaccine is of no use in prevention of cholera.

5. Plan of action
An overall design that meets the objective.

Definition of the population
Country, state, district, town, village, families, or a specific age-income-occupation group. State who is included and who is excluded.

Selection of the sample
Unbiased and large enough to represent the population, within resources. Use a probability technique whenever population inference is intended. Compute n from earlier mean, SD, or proportion; otherwise run a pilot.

Specifying the nature of the study
- Epidemiological field studies are less biased than hospital series unless the objective is deliberately hospital-based. Do not infer rural infant mortality from a teaching-hospital ward.
- Longitudinal: follow a population over time. Prospective follow-up is slower and less biased. Retrospective enquiry is cheaper and quicker but depends on memory.
- Cohort: a group sharing an exposure or a starting event (newborns; women 15–45 years; radiation workers). May be prospective or retrospective.
- Interventional: three phases: identification, intervention for a defined period, assessment of results.
- Experimental / trial: a drug or service is applied and results are watched.
- Cross-sectional (non-experimental): one-time study of a representative sample (nutrition grades in children 0–6 years; point prevalence of pregnancy). Census and many field surveys are cross-sectional. Cover the area piece by piece without returning to households already done.
- Record person, place, and time for every comparison.

Controls
Most experiments need a control as a yardstick. Very rarely it may be unethical to withhold proven life-saving treatment (rabies; antibiotics in lobar pneumonia). A control should be identical to the experimental group in all respects **except the factor under study**. Compare like with like.

Have-nots form a control for haves: cancer in smokers versus cancer in non-smokers. Vitamins A and D given to one group of children and not to a similar group: the second group is the control. Different doses or different durations of tuberculosis chemotherapy may be compared with each other rather than with a no-treatment arm.

Single-blind trial: the patient does not know whether the tablet is drug or placebo (same colour and size).

Double-blind trial: neither the patient nor the bedside observer knows which coded pack is active. The principal investigator labels packs A and B. Essential when the outcome is subjective (pain, sleep).

Allocate to treatment and control with random numbers.

Ruling out observer and instrument error
Train observers. Watch for parallax. Check the weighing machine, sphygmomanometer, stadiometer, and thermometer before use. This precaution is often ignored.

Recording of data
Prepare a standard proforma and **pretest** it. Train interviewers.
- Be friendly; promise confidentiality.
- Questions must be unambiguous (age at last birthday versus nearest birthday).
- Avoid leading or embarrassing direct questions.
- Prefer simple answers: yes, no, do not know.
- Do not ask too many items; extra persons in the room hinder honesty.
- Open-ended items leave space for a free reply. Close-ended items offer coded choices (home or hospital birth; dai, nurse, or doctor).
- State the required accuracy (whole kilograms, one decimal).
- Precodes (Male = 1, Female = 2) speed later computing.

Work schedule
Estimate work per hour and per team from the pilot. Build a master table: one line per person.

6. Presentation of data
Compile, check accuracy and completeness, classify, tabulate, and draw diagrams suited to the data type.

7. Unbiased statistical analysis
Apply the methods of biostatistics without a preconceived result. Involve someone trained in medical statistics.

8. Conclusion
See whether the hypothesis stands. Recheck the whole plan before recommendations, a thesis, or a paper.

Computers help storage, tabulation, and error-free arithmetic. They do not choose the right test.

FOUR STAGES OF A STUDY (SUMMARY)

1. Collection of data (all steps up to the plan of action).
2. Compilation, sorting, and presentation.
3. Analysis by statistical methods.
4. Interpretation, conclusions, recommendations, and publication.

PRESENTATION BRIEFS (THESIS OR PAPER)

1. Title; authors
2. Abstract: brief work, major results
3. Introduction: nature of the problem, reasons, hypothesis, aims
4. Review of literature
5. Materials and methods (the plan of action)
6. Results (presentation of data)
7. Analysis of the results
8. Discussion: compare with literature; state pitfalls
9. Conclusions
10. Recommendations
11. Acknowledgements
12. References
13. Appendices if needed

> **EXAM TIP:** Number the steps: problem → objectives → literature → hypothesis → plan (population, sample, design, control, blinding, proforma) → present → analyse → conclude. Devote a paragraph to matching controls and double-blind trials. Close with "statistician from the start."

KEY POINTS

- Design first; analysis cannot rescue a biased sample.
- Define population, then sample, then control.
- Prospective cohort is slower and cleaner; retrospective is faster and more biased.
- Double-blind plus random allocation protects subjective trials.
- Pretest the proforma. Calibrate instruments. Train observers.
