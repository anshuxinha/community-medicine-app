OVERVIEW

A sample mean or proportion is not the population value. Repeated samples from the same universe give slightly different statistics. That fluctuation is **sampling variation**. Tests of significance and confidence intervals exist to decide whether an observed difference is likely to be this chance variation, or is large enough to reject the idea of "no real difference."

SAMPLING DISTRIBUTION AND STANDARD ERROR

The sampling distribution of the mean is the distribution of x-bar values obtained from many samples of size n drawn from the same population. If n is reasonably large, this sampling distribution is approximately normal even when the parent distribution is not perfectly normal (central limit idea as taught for large samples).

Standard error of the mean (SEM) measures the spread of sample means around the population mean:

SEM = SD / √n

It shrinks as n rises. It is **not** the same as SD (SD is spread of individual observations inside one sample).

Standard error of a proportion:

SE(p) = √(pq / n)

where p is the sample proportion and q = 1 − p (or 100 − p if p is a percent).

HYPOTHESIS TESTING

Hypothesis (H): an assumption about the status of a phenomenon.

Null hypothesis (H0)
The hypothesis of **no difference** (or no association) beyond chance. It is the working assumption the test tries to disprove.
- Research claim: the new treatment differs from (or is better than) the old.
- H0: there is no real difference between the two treatments; any observed difference is a chance finding.

Alternative hypothesis (H1 or Ha)
The hypothesis of a real difference. It may be two-sided (different in either direction) or one-sided when only one direction is scientifically plausible.

STEPS (exam scaffold)
1. State H0 and H1.
2. Choose the test and the significance level α (usually 0.05).
3. Compute the test statistic (Z, t, χ², F) and the p-value, or compare with a critical table value.
4. If p < α (or |statistic| exceeds the critical value), reject H0.
5. Interpret in words: statistically significant is not the same as clinically important, and is not causation.

P-VALUE

The p-value is the probability, **assuming H0 is true**, of obtaining a result as extreme as (or more extreme than) the one observed.

- p > 0.05: not significant at the 5 percent level. Do not reject H0.
- p < 0.05: significant at the 5 percent level. Reject H0.
- p = 0.01: a result this extreme would occur about 1 time in 100 if H0 were true.

Changing the confidence level changes the critical bar:
- Moving from a 95 percent to a 68 percent interval makes it easier to call a difference "significant."
- Moving from 95 percent to 99 percent makes it harder.

Never write "p proves the treatment works." Write "the difference is unlikely under H0 at the stated α."

STATISTICAL ERRORS

|  | H0 true in nature | H0 false in nature |
|---|---|---|
| Reject H0 | Type I error (α, false positive) | Correct (power) |
| Do not reject H0 | Correct | Type II error (β, false negative) |

Type I error (alpha error)
- H0 is true but is rejected.
- Mnemonic: NTR 1 (Null True, Rejected = Type I).
- Its maximum tolerated probability is the significance level α, usually 0.05.
- In medical studies a Type I error is often considered more serious than a Type II error (declaring a useless drug effective).
- Analogous to a false-positive error rate.

Type II error (beta error)
- H0 is false but is accepted (not rejected).
- Mnemonic: NFA 2 (Null False, Accepted = Type II).
- Analogous to a false-negative error.

There is no equation "1 = Alpha." The two identities to remember are:
- Probability of Type I error (when H0 is true) = α
- Power = 1 − β

POWER OF A TEST

Power is the probability of correctly rejecting a false null hypothesis. It is the ability of the test to detect a real difference.

Power = 1 − β

Power rises when:
- The true effect is larger.
- n is larger.
- The SD is smaller.
- α is larger (a more lenient bar; this increases Type I risk).

CONFIDENCE INTERVALS (CI)

A confidence interval is a range, calculated from the sample, that is likely to include the unknown population parameter at a stated confidence level (commonly 95 percent).

Large-sample CI for a mean:

CI = x-bar ± Z × SEM
SEM = SD / √n

| Confidence | Z (two-sided) | Everyday teaching |
|---|---|---|
| 68.27 percent | 1 | mean ± 1 SE |
| 95 percent | 1.96 | often taught as mean ± 2 SE |
| 99 percent | 2.58 | mean ± 2.58 SE |

Interpretation (classical)
If the study were repeated many times, about 95 percent of such intervals would contain the true population value. Do not say "there is a 95 percent probability that this particular interval contains the mean" in a frequentist exam answer; use the long-run wording.

Width of the CI shrinks when n rises or SD falls. A 95 percent CI for a difference that excludes 0 (or for a ratio that excludes 1) usually agrees with p < 0.05.

CI versus p
- p: evidence against H0.
- CI: range of plausible effect sizes; preferred for reporting magnitude.

Example structure
Mean body temperature 98.14 °F, SD 0.6, n = 25.
SEM = 0.6 / √25 = 0.12.
Approximate 95 percent limits: 98.14 ± 1.96 × 0.12 ≈ 97.90 to 98.38 °F.

LEVEL OF SIGNIFICANCE

The conventional 5 percent level (α = 0.05) means: we accept a 5 percent risk of rejecting a true H0. Other levels (1 percent, 0.1 percent) are used when a false positive would be especially costly.

> **EXAM TIP:** Define H0 as no difference. Then SEM formula, then p < 0.05 rule, then the 2 × 2 error table, then power = 1 − β, then CI = mean ± 1.96 SE. One line: SD is within-sample scatter; SE is from-sample-to-sample scatter.

KEY POINTS

- Sampling distribution of the mean has SE = SD / √n.
- H0 is the hypothesis of chance only.
- Type I = α = false positive. Type II = β = false negative. Power = 1 − β.
- 95 percent CI for a mean ≈ x-bar ± 1.96 SE.
- Statistical significance is not clinical importance.

FORMULAS AND CALCULATIONS

- SEM = SD / √n
- SE(p) = √(pq / n)
- Z (for a mean) = (x-bar − μ0) / SEM
- 95 percent CI (mean) = x-bar ± 1.96 × SEM
- 95 percent CI (proportion) = p ± 1.96 × SE(p)
- Power = 1 − β
