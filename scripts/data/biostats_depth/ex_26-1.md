SOLVED EXERCISES

[EX]1. Discuss briefly the application and usefulness of methods in biostatistics in different disciplines of medicine, in a general way.[/EX]
[ANS]
**Definition.** Biostatistics is the application of statistical methods to data derived from biological sciences, especially medicine (quantitative medicine).

**Why it is useful.** Everything in medicine that is to be compared or judged (blood pressure, attack rate, drug effect) must be expressed in figures. Figures vary from person to person. Methods in biostatistics decide whether that variation is chance (biological) or due to a real factor.

**By discipline**
- **Anatomy and physiology:** define normality and its limits (pulse 72 per minute is a mean, not a single "normal" number); compare means of height or blood pressure between places or years; correlate height with weight.
- **Pharmacology:** decide whether a change after a drug is chance; compare two drugs or two doses; find relative potency of a new drug against a standard.
- **Clinical medicine:** compare cure, relief, or death rates in treated versus control groups; test association (smoking and cancer); decide which signs are characteristic of a syndrome.
- **Community medicine:** test sera and vaccines in the field; test a causative factor (iodized salt and goitre); evaluate a programme (pasteurization and typhoid morbidity; family planning and birth rate).

**As figures (vital and health statistics).** They tell leading causes of death and sickness, whether a disease is rising or falling, who is affected, the age-sex structure, and which programme should have priority. They are the eyes and ears of the health administrator.

**Close.** He who accepts statistics indiscriminately will be duped; he who distrusts them indiscriminately will remain ignorant. The student must both compute and interpret.
[/ANS]

[EX]2. Write short notes on: (iv) quantitative data; (v) qualitative data.[/EX]
[ANS]
**(v) Qualitative (categorical) data**
- No notion of metric magnitude; the attribute is named, not measured.
- Persons sharing an attribute form a class: attacked, died, vaccinated, blood group A.
- Only the frequency varies. Results are a ratio, proportion, percentage, or rate.
- Typical tools: standard error of proportion, chi-square test.
- Examples: sex, ABO group, treated versus not treated.

**(iv) Quantitative (numerical) data**
- The characteristic has magnitude. It is counted (discrete) or measured (continuous).
- Discrete examples: parity, number of children, number of attacks.
- Continuous examples: temperature, haemoglobin, birth weight, serum cholesterol.
- Typical tools: mean, standard deviation, standard error of the mean, t and Z tests, correlation.

**Contrast line.** Qualitative = named categories. Quantitative = numbers. Discrete versus continuous is a further split of quantitative data, not a synonym of qualitative versus quantitative.
[/ANS]
