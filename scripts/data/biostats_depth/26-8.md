OVERVIEW

A census counts everyone. A sample studies a representative part when a full count is unnecessary, costly, or impossible. The quality of every later mean, proportion, and p-value depends on how that sample was chosen.

SAMPLING

Sampling is the selection of a number of subjects (the sample) from a defined population in order to estimate characteristics of that population. The **sampling frame** is the list or map of units from which the sample is actually drawn. The frame should match the population as closely as possible; it is not automatically the same thing as the population.

Sample size depends on:
- Effect size you wish to detect
- Standard deviation (or expected proportion)
- Power of the test (1 − β)
- Significance level (α)

Increasing n:
- Does **not** change the pre-set Type I error rate α (that level is chosen before the test)
- Decreases Type II error (β) and therefore **increases power**
- Decreases the standard error of the mean
- Narrows the confidence interval

The sample must be unbiased and large enough for valid inference, yet within money, staff, material, and time. If population SD or P is unknown, do a pilot study.

SAMPLE CHARACTERISTICS

A good sample is:
- Representative of the defined population
- Selected by a known chance mechanism (probability sample) whenever inference to the population is intended
- Large enough that SE is acceptable
- Free, as far as possible, of selection bias, non-response, and measurement error

Bias is especially likely in:
- Retrospective enquiry (memory)
- Subjective answers given to please the doctor
- Non-random (convenience) samples applied as if they were random

TYPES OF SAMPLING

1. Random (probability, non-purposive) sampling
Every unit has a definite, known chance of inclusion.

**Mnemonic:** Random MSC SMS
- M: Multistage
- S: Simple random
- C: Cluster
- S: Systematic
- M: Multiphase
- S: Stratified

Simple random (unrestricted)
- Ideal for small, relatively homogeneous populations.
- Every unit has an equal chance.
- Methods: lottery, computer random numbers, random number tables.
- Common in clinical trials for allocating treatments.

Systematic random
- For large lists. Sampling interval K = N / n. Start at a random number between 1 and K, then take every Kth unit.
- Example: from 1,000 people take 20; K = 50. If the random start is 7, take 7, 57, 107, …

Stratified random
- For a non-homogeneous population. Split into homogeneous strata (age, religion, rural or urban), then sample within each stratum, usually in proportion to stratum size.
- Example: population 1,00,000 with 70,000 Hindus, 20,000 Muslims, 5,000 Christians, 5,000 Sikhs. A sample of 1,000 draws 700, 200, 50, and 50.

Multistage random
- Successive nested draws: state → district → village → person; or school → section → student.
- Used in large country surveys.

Multiphase random
- Cheap information from a large sample; detailed tests on a sub-sample.
- Tuberculosis example: tuberculin in phase 1, X-ray on positives, sputum on X-ray positives.

Cluster random
- The sampling unit is a group (village, school, street), not a person.
- Two-stage idea: list clusters, draw a sample of clusters, then study all or a sample of individuals inside them.
- Cheaper in the field when no complete individual frame exists.
- People inside a cluster are more alike, so a **design effect** is used and the required n is larger than for simple random sampling.
- Classic public health application: evaluation of immunization coverage. World Health Organization (WHO) 30 × 7 technique: 30 clusters, 7 children aged 12–23 months in each cluster (210 children), classified as fully, partially, or unimmunized.

2. Non-random (non-probability, purposive) sampling
**Mnemonic:** Q C PG AS
- Q: Quota
- C: Convenience
- P: Purposive (judgmental)
- G: Grab
- A: Area (grid)
- S: Snowball

Convenience: whoever is easy to reach (mall intercept).
Quota: fill pre-set numbers in subgroups, but choose people non-randomly inside the quota.
Snowball: current subjects recruit hidden contacts (drug users, sex workers).
Purposive / judgmental: the investigator picks "typical" cases (hypertension clinic day).
Grab: volunteers who walk into a camp.
Area: a map grid; squares numbered and sampled.

These samples cannot support the same population inference as probability samples.

ERRORS IN SAMPLING

1. Sampling error
Results differ from sample to sample because only a part was studied. Falls as n rises and as natural variability falls. Measured by the standard error.

2. Non-sampling error
Outside the sampling process: poorly calibrated instruments, observer variation, conceptual error, non-response, faulty estimation. Often larger than sampling error in field surveys.

CENSUS

A census is the process of collecting, compiling, and publishing demographic, economic, and social data pertaining to all persons in a country or delimited territory at a specified time.

In India the first synchronous census in the modern series is dated 1881 (an earlier 1872 count was not fully comparable). Thereafter it has been taken every ten years. The last completed full Census of India was in 2011. The 2021 census was delayed; until a new full census is published, 2011 remains the last completed decennial census. Enumeration is classically in the first quarter of the first year of the decade, when more people are at home.

Legal basis: Census Act, 1948. The Census Commissioner of India (the Registrar General of India holds this charge) directs operations.

Uses in health
- Provides the population denominator for birth, death, and morbidity rates.
- Age-sex composition for planning services.
- Average intercensal growth.
- Frame for samples (address register).

Types
- De facto: persons actually present on census night (enumerated population).
- De jure: usual residents (resident population).

Communal establishments (hostels, prisons, persons working in another city) are hard to enumerate in a single household.

NOTIFIABLE DISEASES

Diseases considered a serious threat to public health are placed on a notifiable list so that prevention and control can start early. Notification supplies morbidity data, fluctuation in frequency, and early warning of outbreaks.

Under the International Health Regulations, certain diseases are notifiable in the international system (historically including plague, yellow fever, and cholera; poliomyelitis remains a public-health emergency of international concern framework disease). National lists are wider (for example influenza, malaria, rabies, salmonellosis, louse-borne typhus, relapsing fever, and others as notified by the state).

Limitations
- Atypical and subclinical cases escape recognition (rubella, non-paralytic polio).
- Under-reporting is heavy.
- Notification covers only a small part of total community sickness.

KEY POINTS

- The sampling frame is the list you draw from; it should represent the population but is not identical to it.
- Probability samples support inference; convenience samples do not.
- Stratify when groups differ. Cluster when the field is wide and no name-list exists.
- Design effect inflates n in cluster samples.
- SE falls as n rises. Non-sampling error needs training and calibrated tools.
- Census is a full count; 2011 is the last completed Indian decennial census.

FORMULAS AND CALCULATIONS

- K (sampling interval) = N / n
- SEM = SD / √n
- Cluster coverage example: 30 clusters × 7 children = 210
