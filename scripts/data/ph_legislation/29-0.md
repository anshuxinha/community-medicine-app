OVERVIEW OF THE CHAPTER
Public health legislation is the body of statutes, rules, and codes through which the State converts health policy into enforceable duties. A resident who can only name programmes cannot answer Paper III. The expected answer is a classification, then provisions of named Acts, then why each Act protects population health.

This leaf is the examination skeleton. Later leaves write the individual statutes in MD short-note and long-answer depth. Clinical and programme detail already taught elsewhere (Medical Termination of Pregnancy methods in family planning, Cigarettes and Other Tobacco Products Act operations under the National Tobacco Control Programme, Bio-Medical Waste colour coding in Hospital Waste Management, occupational hygiene in Occupational Health) stays there. This chapter is the legal home.

DEFINITIONS
PUBLIC HEALTH LAW: Rules of the State that regulate individual and community behaviour in order to protect and promote health, prevent disease and injury, and organise health services. Custom may seed a law (for example marriage custom), but public health statutes are enacted by Parliament or State Legislatures and implemented through notified rules.

ACT: Primary legislation passed by a legislature and assented to by the President or Governor.

RULES AND REGULATIONS: Subordinate legislation framed under an Act by the executive (Central or State Government, or a statutory board) to make the Act operational. Examination answers that name only the year of the Act and skip the Rules lose marks on “how it works”.

CODE: A consolidating statute that repeals and replaces several older Acts in one field. India’s four labour codes (Code on Wages, 2019; Industrial Relations Code, 2020; Code on Social Security, 2020; Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code, 2020) were brought into force from 21 November 2025.

ORDINANCE: Temporary law made by the President or Governor when the legislature is not in session; it must be laid before the House.

INTERNATIONAL HEALTH REGULATIONS (IHR) 2005: A binding international legal instrument under the World Health Organization (WHO) that sets how States detect, notify, and respond to public health emergencies of international concern. Not an Indian Act, but examiners pair it with the Epidemic Diseases Act, 1897 and the Disaster Management Act, 2005.

CONSTITUTIONAL BASIS
Health is not named as a fundamental right in so many words, but the Supreme Court has read a right to health into Article 21 (right to life and personal liberty). Directive Principles that examiners expect:

- Article 38: social order for the welfare of the people.
- Article 39: health of workers, and that children are not abused.
- Article 42: just and humane conditions of work and maternity relief.
- Article 47: duty of the State to raise the level of nutrition and the standard of living and to improve public health; in particular, to bring about prohibition of intoxicating drinks and drugs injurious to health.

Fundamental rights that statutes must respect: Article 14 (equality), Article 15 (non-discrimination), Article 21 (life and personal liberty, including emergency medical care and, in later jurisprudence, dignity in mental health and disability).

SEVENTH SCHEDULE (WHO MAKES THE LAW)
- Union List: census, inter-state migration, port quarantine, patents, opium (selected entries).
- State List (Entry 6): public health and sanitation, hospitals and dispensaries. This is why many health statutes need State adoption or State Rules (Clinical Establishments Act, 2010 is the classic example).
- Concurrent List: vital statistics including registration of births and deaths, population control and family planning, labour welfare, drugs, prevention of infectious or contagious diseases spreading from one State to another, factories.

A clean one-liner for viva: public health is a State subject; many modern health laws are Central Acts on the Concurrent List, with State Rules for enforcement.

EIGHT-BUCKET CLASSIFICATION FOR A LONG ANSWER
Use this as the heading order when the question is “various public health legislations”. Fill two to four named Acts under each bucket. Do not dump an ungrouped list of twenty years.

1. Reproductive, maternal, and sex-selection law: Medical Termination of Pregnancy Act, 1971 (Amendment 2021); Pre-Conception and Pre-Natal Diagnostic Techniques (Prohibition of Sex Selection) Act, 1994; Surrogacy (Regulation) Act, 2021; Assisted Reproductive Technology (Regulation) Act, 2021.
2. Child protection, feeding, and education: Protection of Children from Sexual Offences Act, 2012; Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015; Child and Adolescent Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act, 1986 as amended 2016; Prohibition of Child Marriage Act, 2006; Infant Milk Substitutes, Feeding Bottles and Infant Foods (Regulation of Production, Supply and Distribution) Act, 1992; Right of Children to Free and Compulsory Education Act, 2009.
3. Women: maternity, violence, traffic, dowry: Maternity Benefit Act, 1961 (Amendment 2017); Protection of Women from Domestic Violence Act, 2005; Dowry Prohibition Act, 1961; Immoral Traffic (Prevention) Act, 1956.
4. Mental health, disability, HIV, transgender persons: Mental Healthcare Act, 2017; Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016; Human Immunodeficiency Virus and Acquired Immune Deficiency Syndrome (Prevention and Control) Act, 2017; Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act, 2019.
5. Food, drugs, tobacco, narcotics: Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006; National Food Security Act, 2013; Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940; Cigarettes and Other Tobacco Products Act, 2003; Narcotic Drugs and Psychotropic Substances Act, 1985.
6. Epidemics, disasters, international health: Epidemic Diseases Act, 1897 (Amendment 2020); Disaster Management Act, 2005; International Health Regulations, 2005.
7. Occupational health and labour: classic answers still name the Factories Act, 1948 and the Employees’ State Insurance Act, 1948. Current umbrella: Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code, 2020 and Code on Social Security, 2020, in force from 21 November 2025 (Central Rules notified May 2026). During transition, relevant provisions of the older Acts and their rules remain in force where corresponding Code rules are not yet operational.
8. Environment and waste; professional regulation and consumer; vital statistics; transplantation and related: Environment (Protection) Act, 1986; Bio-Medical Waste Management Rules, 2016; National Medical Commission Act, 2019; Clinical Establishments (Registration and Regulation) Act, 2010; Consumer Protection Act, 2019; Registration of Births and Deaths Act, 1969 (Amendment 2023); Transplantation of Human Organs and Tissues Act, 1994.

OLDER NINE-ACT TEACHING LIST (STILL ASKED IN VIVA)
Many theory papers still expect this classic list, then the update:

1. Prevention of Food Adulteration Act, 1954: repealed; replaced by the Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006 (in force 2011).
2. Medical Termination of Pregnancy Act, 1971: still in force; Amendment Act 2021 changed gestational limits and opinions.
3. Employees’ State Insurance Act, 1948: still taught; social-security functions now sit under the Code on Social Security, 2020.
4. Factories Act, 1948: still taught; safety and working conditions now sit under the Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code, 2020.
5. Maternity Benefit Act, 1961: 26 weeks after the 2017 amendment; also mapped into the Social Security Code.
6. Children Act, 1960: superseded by the Juvenile Justice (Care and Protection of Children) Act, 2015.
7. Registration of Births and Deaths Act, 1969: still in force; Amendment Act 2023 created a national database and made the birth certificate a single document for specified purposes.
8. Epidemic Diseases Act, 1897: still in force; Amendment Act 2020 added protection of health-care service personnel.
9. Juvenile Justice Act, 1986: superseded by the 2015 Act.

HOW TO WRITE THE LONG ANSWER
1. One-sentence definition of public health legislation.
2. Constitutional peg (Articles 21 and 47; State List versus Concurrent List).
3. Classification table (the eight buckets, or a shorter four-way split: reproductive, occupational, food and drugs, environment and epidemics).
4. For each heading, two Acts with year, object, two or three operative provisions, and the public-health purpose.
5. One line on labour codes (2025) so the answer is not frozen in 1948.
6. Close: legislation is a primary-prevention tool (tobacco, food, environment) and a rights tool (mental health, disability, HIV). Enforcement and State Rules decide whether the statute reaches the last mile.

WHAT NOT TO DO
- Do not recite only years.
- Do not write the Prevention of Food Adulteration Act, 1954 or the Mental Health Act, 1987 as if they were current.
- Do not use the obsolete Medical Termination of Pregnancy opinion rule (one doctor up to 12 weeks, two doctors 12 to 20 weeks). Current law is one Registered Medical Practitioner up to 20 weeks.
- Do not tell the examiner to “check the latest circular”. The provisions in the later leaves are the current examination text.

> **EXAM TIP:** Open with a definition and Articles 21 and 47. Draw the eight-bucket table (or a four-row table if time is short). Under each row name two Acts with year plus two provisions. End with one sentence on labour codes (21 November 2025) and one sentence that law without State Rules and inspection does not protect health.
