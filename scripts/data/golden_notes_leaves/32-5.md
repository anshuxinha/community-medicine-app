OVERVIEW OF THE CHAPTER
Bioterrorism is the deliberate release of biological agents to harm people, animals, or plants. Biosafety protects workers and the community from accidental exposure. Biosecurity prevents theft, diversion, and deliberate misuse.

DEFINITIONS
BIOTERRORISM: Intentional use of microorganisms or toxins to cause disease, death, or panic.
BIOSAFETY: Containment principles, technologies, and practices that prevent unintentional exposure to biological agents.
BIOSECURITY: Institutional and national measures that prevent loss, theft, misuse, diversion, or intentional release of biological agents and toxins.

[LAQ]Public health response to a suspected biological attack[/LAQ]

AGENTS (CDC CATEGORY TEACHING)
- Category A: anthrax, plague, smallpox, botulism, tularaemia, viral haemorrhagic fevers. High mortality, panic, need for special preparedness.
- Category B: brucella, Q fever, ricin, food-safety threats. Moderate.
- Category C: emerging pathogens that could be engineered.

RESPONSE
1. Recognise: unusual clustering, unexpected age group, aerosol-looking anthrax, haemorrhagic fever without travel.
2. Isolate and PPE. Notify IHIP / NCDC / IHR National Focal Point (NCDC).
3. Laboratory: designated biosafety level. Do not process unknown powders in a routine clinical lab.
4. Medical countermeasures: antibiotics or vaccine as per agent protocol (anthrax PEP is the classic).
5. Risk communication: one official voice. Avoid rumour.
6. Law: Epidemic Diseases Act, Disaster Management Act, Unlawful Activities framework as advised by the home ministry. IHR 2005 if international spread is possible.

INDIAN CONTEXT:
- Smallpox remains an IHR immediately notifiable disease even though it is eradicated. Anthrax is both a zoonosis and a bioterrorism category A agent. Biosafety versus biosecurity is a favourite contrast pair (8.86 in the notes book).
