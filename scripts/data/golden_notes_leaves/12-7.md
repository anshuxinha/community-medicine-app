OVERVIEW OF THE CHAPTER
This section covers four social-science topics that sit beside sociology and psychology in Community Medicine: anthropology and public health, social marketing, child abuse, and the World Health Organization (WHO) community-based rehabilitation (CBR) matrix. Motivation theory (Maslow) remains with leadership. Suicide epidemiology remains in the mental-health section.

DEFINITIONS
ANTHROPOLOGY: The science of human beings: origin, biology, culture, beliefs, and social customs. The term joins anthropos (human) and logos (discourse). The Concise Oxford wording is the study of mankind, especially of its societies and customs, and of the structure and evolution of man as an animal.
SOCIAL MARKETING: The systematic application of marketing concepts and techniques to achieve specific behavioural goals for a social good. Kotler and related teaching define it as applying commercial marketing principles and techniques to create, communicate, and deliver value in order to influence target-audience behaviours that benefit society (public health, safety, the environment, and communities) as well as the audience.
CHILD ABUSE (WHO, 1999): All forms of physical and/or emotional ill-treatment, sexual abuse, neglect or negligent treatment, or commercial or other exploitation, resulting in actual or potential harm to the child's health, survival, development, or dignity in the context of a relationship of responsibility, trust, or power.
COMMUNITY-BASED REHABILITATION: A strategy within community development for the rehabilitation, equalization of opportunities, poverty reduction, and social inclusion of people with disabilities, carried out through the combined efforts of people with disabilities, their families, organisations, and communities, and the relevant government and non-government services.

ANTHROPOLOGY AND PUBLIC HEALTH
Major branches: social or cultural anthropology; physical or biological anthropology; archaeological anthropology; linguistic anthropology; and medical anthropology, which emerged as a sub-discipline in the 1950s.

Social anthropology studies what people make, do, and think, and how they organise social relationships. Fieldwork (living with a community, observing, and participating) produces ethnographies. Comparison across societies tests generalisations about human nature.

Medical anthropology studies health and disease, health-care systems, and biocultural adaptation. Theoretical approaches include:
- Ecological: how culture and the natural environment interact to create patterns of health and disease.
- Interpretive: how people use symbol and meaning to describe illness.
- Critical: how economics and politics shape who falls ill and who receives care.

Methods that public health borrows include ethnography, qualitative interviews, and mixed qualitative-quantitative designs. Problems such as human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) risk, psychiatric distress, and alcohol use are often not captured by a single cross-sectional survey. They require field observation of belief, myth, and practice.

Public-health interventions that ignore grass-root realities fail at acceptability (the Tanahashi stage). Anthropology supplies that local reading: who decides on hospital delivery, what a fever is called, which foods are withheld after childbirth.

SOCIAL MARKETING
Social marketing is distinct from commercial marketing because it sells ideas and behaviours for social welfare, not a private profit. It does not favour free distribution that produces waste. It argues for scale, sustainability, and accountability.

Benchmark criteria commonly listed:
1. Customer orientation.
2. Focus on behaviour, not only awareness.
3. Use of behavioural theory.
4. Insight into the audience's barriers and motives.
5. Exchange: the audience must see a benefit that outweighs cost.
6. Competition: other behaviours (inaction, harmful products) are acknowledged.
7. Segmentation of audiences.
8. Mix of methods (the marketing mix).

The marketing mix in health teaching is often six Ps: product, price, place, promotion, partnership, and policy.

Indian examples:
- Deluxe Nirodh (lubricated condoms) and Mala-D (combined oral pills) under contraceptive social marketing.
- Saheli (centchroman, a non-steroidal oral contraceptive).
- Iodized salt, oral rehydration salt (ORS), zinc, and sanitary napkins where social-marketing channels are used.
- Pulse Polio ("Do Boond Zindagi Ki").
- Red Ribbon campaign under the National AIDS Control Programme (NACP).

Obstacles include cultural and religious resistance, low literacy, and price constraints. Social marketing is not the same as a PPP hospital contract; it is a demand-side method for a product or behaviour.

CHILD ABUSE
WHO (1999) places abuse in a relationship of responsibility, trust, or power. Types taught:
- Physical abuse: intentional use of physical force that results in, or has a high likelihood of resulting in, harm.
- Sexual abuse: involvement of a child in sexual activity that the child does not fully comprehend, cannot consent to, or for which the child is not developmentally prepared.
- Emotional or psychological abuse: isolating, threatening, rejecting, or other acts that harm emotional development.
- Neglect: failure to provide health, education, emotional development, nutrition, shelter, and safe living conditions.

Risk factors cluster in caregiver stress, poverty, substance use, social isolation, and the child's disability or unwanted status. Health consequences include injury, impaired growth and development, mental illness, high-risk sexual behaviour later, and death. In India, the Protection of Children from Sexual Offences (POCSO) Act, 2012, the Juvenile Justice Act, 2015, and childline 1098 are the legal and service pegs. Battered-baby syndrome in the paediatric section is the clinical extreme of physical abuse; this section is the public-health frame.

COMMUNITY-BASED REHABILITATION MATRIX
WHO presents CBR as a matrix of five components against five levels of society:
- Components: health; education; livelihood; social; empowerment.
- Levels: the person; the family; the community; and wider society (and, in some tables, policy).

Health includes promotion, prevention, medical care, rehabilitation, and assistive devices. Education includes early childhood, primary to higher education, and non-formal and lifelong learning. Livelihood includes skills, self-employment, wage employment, and social protection. Social includes relationships, family, culture, recreation, and justice. Empowerment includes advocacy, communication, social mobilisation, political participation, and self-help groups.

The matrix is a planning grid, not a building. A district disability plan should show an activity in more than one cell (for example, a hearing aid is health; school placement is education; a disability pension is livelihood/social protection).

INDIAN CONTEXT:
The Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act, 2016, and National Programme for Prevention and Control of Deafness (NPPCD), National Programme for Control of Blindness and Visual Impairment (NPCBVI), and Rashtriya Bal Swasthya Karyakram (RBSK) District Early Intervention Centres are the usual service links to CBR. Social marketing of contraceptives sits with the National Family Welfare Programme. Child abuse reporting sits with the Integrated Child Protection Scheme and POCSO.
