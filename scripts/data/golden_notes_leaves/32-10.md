OVERVIEW OF THE CHAPTER
Three Paper IV miscellaneous topics: medical tourism (medical value travel), carbon footprint of health care, and genetically modified (GM) foods.

DEFINITIONS
MEDICAL TOURISM: Travel across international borders to obtain medical care, usually combining treatment with a stay in the destination country.
CARBON FOOTPRINT: The total greenhouse-gas emissions caused directly and indirectly by an activity, person, organisation, or product, expressed as carbon dioxide equivalent.
GENETICALLY MODIFIED FOOD: Food derived from an organism whose genetic material has been altered by modern biotechnology in a way that does not occur by mating or natural recombination.

[SN]Medical tourism[/SN]

MEDICAL TOURISM
- India attracts patients for elective surgery, oncology, cardiology, transplants, and wellness, citing cost, English-speaking clinicians, and private-hospital capacity.
- Public-health angles: foreign-exchange earnings; pressure on specialist time; need for quality regulation (Clinical Establishments Act, NABH); infection control and antimicrobial stewardship for inbound patients; follow-up after the patient leaves.
- Risks: unethical transplant tourism (Transplantation of Human Organs and Tissues Act), antibiotic-resistant organisms travelling with patients, and neglect of domestic primary care if policy chases only high-end private work.

[SN]Carbon footprint of health care[/SN]

CARBON FOOTPRINT
- Health systems produce a measurable share of national greenhouse-gas emissions (energy, theatres, supply chain, waste, transport).
- Mitigation taught with NPCCHH climate-resilient facilities: efficient buildings, renewable power, rational oxygen and anaesthetic gases, BMW segregation, less unnecessary travel.
- Link the climate and heat-health topic for vectors and heat. This note is the facility carbon line.

[SN]Genetically modified foods[/SN]

GM FOODS
- Examples in global teaching: Bt cotton (India commercial crop, not a staple food); GM soybean and maize in other countries.
- Potential: higher yield, pest resistance, micronutrient biofortification (example: Golden Rice as a teaching case, not an Indian commercial staple).
- Concerns: allergenicity, gene flow to wild relatives, farmer seed dependence, labelling, and public trust.
- Indian regulation: Genetic Engineering Appraisal Committee (GEAC) under the Ministry of Environment; Food Safety and Standards Authority of India (FSSAI) for labelling of GM food. Do not claim a current nationwide commercial GM food staple unless a GEAC approval is named.

INDIAN CONTEXT:
- Medical tourism is a commerce-and-quality question. Carbon footprint is an NPCCHH/green-hospital question. GM food is a regulation-and-nutrition question. Keep the three headings separate in a long answer.

> **EXAM TIP:** Three short notes stacked: definition, two benefits, two risks, one Indian regulator or programme each.
