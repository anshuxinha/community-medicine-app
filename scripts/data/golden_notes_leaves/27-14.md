OVERVIEW OF THE CHAPTER
Public-private partnership (PPP) in health is an arrangement in which government and private actors share roles, investment, risk, and performance standards to deliver a public-health service. It is not a substitute for a functioning public system. It is one method of expanding access, skills, and capital when the public sector cannot do the whole task alone.

DEFINITIONS
PUBLIC-PRIVATE PARTNERSHIP (WHO, 1999): Bringing together a set of actors for the common goal of improving the health of a population, based on mutually agreed roles and principles.
PUBLIC-PRIVATE PARTNERSHIP (Government of India, 2010): An arrangement between a government or statutory entity on one side and a private sector entity on the other, for the provision of public services or related services for public benefit, with investments made and/or management undertaken by the private sector for a specified time period, with well-defined risk sharing, and with performance-linked payments that conform to predetermined, measurable standards.
PRIVATE SECTOR (Bennet, 1991): Organisations working outside the direct control of government. The public sector is funded and controlled by government.

HEALTH-SECTOR REFORM AND THE PLACE OF PPP
The World Health Organization (WHO) described health-sector reform as a sustained process of fundamental change in policy and institutional arrangements of the health sector, usually guided by the government. Reform strategies include alternative financing, institutional management, involvement of local government, contracting in or out, civil-service and public-sector reforms, capacity building, productivity improvement, and collaboration with the private sector.

In India, public expenditure on health remains a small share of Gross Domestic Product (GDP) relative to total health expenditure. A large share of care is already purchased in the private market, much of it as out-of-pocket spending. Partnership is therefore a live administrative question, not a theoretical one.

TYPES OF PRIVATE ACTORS (WORLD BANK TEACHING)
Informal providers: accessible and low-cost; quality is often poor and mainstreaming is difficult.
Not-for-profit organisations: often higher quality and targeted to the poor; coverage is small, resources are limited, and scale-up is hard.
For-profit providers: high quality in selected disciplines, large outreach, and innovation; care is expensive, quality is variable, and facilities cluster in cities.

FOUR Cs OF PPP
Partnership is a combination of communication, consultation, coordination, and collaboration.

OBJECTIVES
- Improve quality, accessibility, availability, acceptability, and efficiency.
- Exchange skills and expertise between public and private sectors.
- Mobilise additional resources.
- Improve allocation of resources and generate additional resources.
- Strengthen management inside government infrastructure.
- Widen the range and number of service providers.
- Share risk under clearly defined rules.
- Support community ownership where the design allows it.

AREAS OF PARTNERSHIP
Community health, hospital services, education, training, and research are the usual domains. Contracting may be for diagnostics, dialysis, ambulance, biomedical-waste treatment, diet, laundry, or selected clinical packages.

KEY DETERMINANTS
Policies, legislation, and procedures; institutional framework; communication; partner selection; a life-cycle approach to the contract; integrity, transparency, accountability, and governance; effective service delivery and user satisfaction.

INDIAN EXAMPLES (TEACHING SET)
- Dialysis under the Pradhan Mantri National Dialysis Programme through contracted providers at district hospitals.
- Emergency transport (108 and related models) in many States.
- Diagnostic and radiology contracts at public hospitals.
- Social franchising and social marketing of contraceptives (Nirodh, Mala-D) as a related but not identical instrument.
- Janani Suraksha Yojana and later schemes that empanel private facilities for delivery, where States use that route.
- Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana (PM-JAY) as strategic purchasing from empaneled public and private hospitals, which is insurance-based purchasing rather than a facility-management PPP.

MONITORING
A PPP needs a written contract, measurable outputs (waiting time, infection rates, dialysis sessions, response time), independent audit, and an exit clause. Performance-linked payment is the Government of India 2010 test: if payment is not tied to standards, the arrangement is a grant or a purchase, not a partnership.

INDIAN CONTEXT:
National Health Mission (NHM) and State health societies are the usual public signatories. Clinical Establishments Act registration, biomedical-waste rules, and professional regulation still apply to the private partner. PPP does not exempt the State from the duty to provide essential care if the partner fails.
