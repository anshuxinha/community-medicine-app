OVERVIEW OF THE CHAPTER
This section covers four tools used in health-team work and programme evaluation: the Johari window, sociometry, the Tanahashi model of health-service coverage, and social audit. Leadership styles and motivation theories remain in the preceding section on leadership and behaviour change.

DEFINITIONS
JOHARI WINDOW: A four-quadrant model of self-awareness and interpersonal communication, named from Joseph Luft and Harry Ingham. It maps what is known or unknown to the self against what is known or unknown to others.
SOCIOMETRY: A method for describing, measuring, and analysing the structure of social relations within a group, including the extent of acceptance among members. The word combines the Latin socius (companion) and metrum (measure).
TANAHASHI MODEL: A framework in which health-service coverage is the ability of a service to interact with the people who should benefit from it, transforming the intention to serve into a completed intervention. Coverage is described at successive stages from availability of resources to effective coverage.
SOCIAL AUDIT: A democratic process in which the community systematically demands information on works and programmes already implemented in its area, and jointly reviews quality, expenditure, and decisions with the implementing agency.

JOHARI WINDOW
The model rests on two ideas: trust grows when people disclose information about themselves, and people improve when they use feedback from others.

The four quadrants are:
1. Open (arena): known to self and known to others. Shared facts, skills, and working habits sit here.
2. Blind: unknown to self, known to others. Colleagues see habits the person does not notice.
3. Hidden (facade): known to self, unknown to others. Private history, doubts, and withheld information sit here.
4. Unknown: unknown to self and unknown to others. Untapped ability or unrecognised strain.

A new team member typically has a small open quadrant. Disclosure enlarges the open area from the hidden quadrant. Feedback enlarges it from the blind quadrant. Training and joint work shrink the unknown quadrant. In a Primary Health Centre (PHC) team, a medical officer who invites comment on clinic organisation reduces the blind area; an Auxiliary Nurse Midwife (ANM) who states a training need reduces the hidden area.

SOCIOMETRY
Moreno described sociometry as a method for measuring the attractions, rejections, and choices people make when they gather. Whenever a group forms, members choose with whom they affiliate. Sociometry makes those choices visible.

Uses in a health team:
- Map who is sought for advice, who is isolated, and who bridges subgroups.
- Improve communication and cohesion before a campaign (immunization week, outbreak response).
- Reduce conflict by showing informal structure that the organogram does not show.

A simple exercise asks each member to name two colleagues they would choose for a difficult field visit. The resulting sociogram shows stars, isolates, and cliques. Supervision then uses the map: isolates need support; stars should not be overloaded; cliques should not capture decisions.

TANAHASHI MODEL OF HEALTH SERVICE COVERAGE
Health-service coverage depends on the ability of a service to interact with the target population. Service capacity is the number of people for whom the service can be provided. Service output is the number who actually receive it. Utilization is the relationship between capacity and output. Potential coverage relates to capacity; actual coverage relates to output.

Five successive stages of coverage are described:
1. Availability coverage: people for whom the service exists, set by manpower, facilities, drugs, and equipment.
2. Accessibility coverage: people who can reach the service (distance, transport, opening hours, cost of travel).
3. Acceptability coverage: people who are willing to use the service (culture, gender of provider, privacy, trust, language).
4. Contact coverage: people who actually use the service (outpatient visits, antenatal contacts, immunization sessions).
5. Effective coverage: people who receive a correctly delivered intervention that achieves the intended health effect (right drug, right dose, completed schedule, controlled blood pressure).

Bottlenecks appear as a drop from one stage to the next. A Community Health Centre (CHC) may have staff (availability) but poor roads (accessibility), or women may refuse a male examiner (acceptability). Programme review names the stage that is leaking and acts there: drugs and posts for availability; transport and timing for accessibility; privacy and female staff for acceptability; outreach for contact; protocols and follow-up for effective coverage.

SOCIAL AUDIT
Social audit is a social-accountability tool. People and the government jointly monitor a project. It covers quality of implementation, use of funds, and the decisions that shaped the work. It is meant to be empowering: the community participates in planning and review, and the implementing agency answers for results.

Major objectives:
- Recognise transparency and accountability as public duties.
- Assess gaps between needs and resources, physical and financial.
- Create awareness among beneficiaries and providers.
- Increase efficacy and effectiveness of the programme.
- Scrutinise policy decisions that affect the locality.
- Sensitise citizens to demand accountability.

Guiding principles commonly taught (Hindi terms as used in Indian toolkits):
- Access to information (Jaankari): equal, open access for all citizens.
- Participation (Bhagidari): involvement in decisions and outcomes.
- Protection of citizens (Suraksha): a secure forum for free discussion, with administrative liaison.
- Right to be heard (Sunwai): grievances addressed and follow-up reported at the next hearing.
- Collective platform (Janta ka manch): a shared space that strengthens citizens' voices.
- Findings made public and accessible.

Steps:
1. Define the boundaries of the audit.
2. Identify and consult stakeholders.
3. Identify key issues; collect and verify data; prepare a report.
4. Organise public meetings.
5. Advocate findings and institutionalise the practice.

In health, Village Health Sanitation and Nutrition Committees (VHSNCs) and Jan Arogya Samitis are the usual local platforms. National rural employment and food programmes also run social-audit units. A medical officer's role is to place facility records, drug stock, and session reports in the public meeting and to act on the gaps named there.

INDIAN CONTEXT:
Johari and sociometry are team tools at PHC and district programme-management unit level. Tanahashi stages are used when a district reviews why immunization or antenatal coverage is low despite posted staff. Social audit sits with Panchayati Raj and National Health Mission (NHM) community platforms.
